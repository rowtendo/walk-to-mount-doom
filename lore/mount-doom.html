The Mountain of Fire

Mount Doom, called Orodruin in Sindarin and also known as Amon Amarth, stood in the northwestern part of Mordor upon the plateau of Gorgoroth. It rose southeast of the Morannon and southwest of Barad-dûr, making it one of the central landmarks of Sauron's realm.

The mountain was an active volcano whose fires played a major role in the history of the Second and Third Ages. Tolkien does not give a detailed geological history of Orodruin, but its eruptions and internal fires were repeatedly associated with Sauron's works and with the power he concentrated in Mordor.

Its importance came not from height or military strength alone. Orodruin was the place where Sauron forged the One Ring, and because that Ring could be destroyed only in the fire where it had been made, the mountain eventually became the ultimate destination of Frodo's quest.

Sauron Chooses Mordor

Around Second Age 1000, Sauron established himself in Mordor and began turning the enclosed country into the center of his power. The surrounding mountain ranges provided strong natural defenses, while Orodruin gave him access to a source of extraordinary heat and fire.

Sauron built Barad-dûr during the following centuries and developed Mordor into a fortified realm. The proximity of Orodruin to his principal stronghold made the mountain directly useful to his plans, especially as his attention turned toward the creation of Rings of Power.

The precise reasons Sauron chose Mordor are not fully explained in a single account, but the combination of isolation, defensibility, and the fires of Orodruin made the region well suited to his purposes. By the middle of the Second Age, Mount Doom had become inseparable from the power he was building there.

The Rings of Power

During the Second Age, Sauron appeared among the Elves of Eregion in a fair form and used the name Annatar, presenting himself as a giver of knowledge. He worked with the Elven-smiths and helped guide the craft that produced many of the Rings of Power.

The greatest of the Elven smiths was Celebrimbor, leader of the Gwaith-i-Mírdain. Under Sauron's influence, the smiths created the lesser rings and the Seven and the Nine, while the Three Rings of the Elves were made by Celebrimbor without Sauron directly touching them.

Sauron's true purpose was to create a ruling Ring that would allow him to dominate the other Rings and those who wore them. To accomplish that, he returned to Mordor and went to the fires of Orodruin.

The Forging of the One Ring

Around Second Age 1600, Sauron forged the One Ring in the fire of Mount Doom. In doing so, he placed a large part of his own native power into the Ring, binding its existence closely to his ability to dominate the other Rings of Power.

The One Ring was therefore not simply a powerful artifact made by Sauron. It contained a substantial investment of his own strength and became central to the structure of his power throughout the remainder of the Second and Third Ages.

Because the Ring was made in Orodruin, ordinary fire could not destroy it. Its destruction required returning it to the same volcanic fire in which it had been forged, a fact that later determined the entire strategy of the Council of Elrond.

The forging of the Ring also revealed Sauron's deception to the Elves. Those who wore the Three perceived him and removed their Rings before he could master them through the One.

War with the Elves

After the Elves understood Sauron's betrayal, war broke out. In Second Age 1695, Sauron invaded Eriador, and in 1697 he destroyed Eregion and killed Celebrimbor.

Sauron recovered many of the Rings of Power and distributed the Seven and the Nine in accordance with his larger plans. The Three, however, remained beyond his control, and the One Ring did not give him the complete mastery he had intended.

The war ultimately turned against him after the intervention of Númenor. Sauron was driven from Eriador, but he retained Mordor, Barad-dûr, and Orodruin as the center of his power.

Mount Doom thus remained the place most closely associated with the Ring even after Sauron's first attempt at domination through it had failed to achieve complete success.

The Last Alliance

At the end of the Second Age, Sauron again went to war against the descendants of Númenor. Elendil and Gil-galad responded by forming the Last Alliance of Elves and Men, gathering a great host to confront him.

The alliance defeated Sauron's forces at Dagorlad and entered Mordor. Barad-dûr was then besieged for seven years, placing the heart of Sauron's realm under sustained attack.

In Second Age 3441, Sauron emerged and fought personally against Gil-galad and Elendil. Both leaders were killed, but Sauron was overthrown, and Isildur cut the One Ring from his hand using the broken sword Narsil.

The Ring that had been forged at Mount Doom was therefore taken from Sauron only a short distance from the place where it could have been destroyed.

Isildur Refuses to Destroy the Ring

After Sauron's defeat, Elrond and Círdan advised Isildur to destroy the Ring. Tolkien later records that Isildur refused and claimed it as compensation for the deaths of his father and brother.

Isildur did not cast the Ring into the fire of Orodruin. Instead, he kept it and left Mordor, intending to preserve it as an heirloom of his house.

This decision allowed the Ring to survive the end of the Second Age. When Isildur was later killed at the Gladden Fields, the Ring slipped from him and disappeared into the Anduin.

Orodruin consequently remained the one place where Sauron's most important work could have been permanently undone, but the opportunity was lost for more than three thousand years.

The Long Silence of the Mountain

After Sauron's defeat, Gondor maintained a watch on Mordor for many centuries. The land was largely deserted, and the kingdom built or occupied fortifications intended to prevent Sauron's servants from returning.

Mount Doom remained within the abandoned realm. Tolkien does not describe continuous activity at the mountain during much of the early Third Age, and its fires were not always visibly active.

As Gondor weakened, however, the watch on Mordor declined. The Nazgûl returned, Sauron's servants reoccupied the land, and the old defenses established by Gondor gradually failed.

Orodruin would become active again as Sauron prepared to restore Mordor as the center of his power.

Sauron Returns to Mordor

After centuries of concealment at Dol Guldur, Sauron returned secretly to Mordor in Third Age 2941. In 2951, he declared himself openly and began rebuilding Barad-dûr.

As Sauron's power grew, Mount Doom became active again. In 2954, Orodruin erupted, and the population of Ithilien was further reduced as fear and instability spread through the region.

The renewed activity of the mountain was closely associated with Sauron's return, though Tolkien does not state that every eruption was directly commanded by him. What is clear is that Mount Doom once again became a visible sign of the restored power of Mordor.

By the time of the War of the Ring, smoke, flame, and volcanic activity around Orodruin dominated the landscape of Gorgoroth.

The Council of Elrond

In Third Age 3018, the true nature of Bilbo's old ring was confirmed, and the One Ring was brought to Rivendell by Frodo Baggins. At the Council of Elrond, representatives of the Free Peoples considered what could be done with it.

The Council rejected the idea of using the Ring against Sauron because anyone attempting to control it would ultimately be corrupted. Hiding it permanently was also considered inadequate because Sauron would continue searching for it.

The only viable course was destruction, and that meant returning the Ring to the fire of Mount Doom. The entire quest was therefore defined by the mountain's unique relationship with the Ring.

Frodo volunteered to carry the Ring, and the Fellowship of the Ring was formed to help him begin the journey.

The Road Toward Mordor

The Fellowship left Rivendell in December 3018 and traveled south. After the Company broke at Amon Hen, Frodo and Samwise Gamgee continued toward Mordor without the rest.

Their journey eventually brought them through the Dead Marshes to the Black Gate, where they discovered that the Morannon was too heavily defended to enter secretly. Gollum then led them south toward Cirith Ungol.

After surviving Shelob's attack and escaping the Tower of Cirith Ungol, Frodo and Sam finally entered Mordor. Their objective was now geographically close but still separated from them by the barren plateau of Gorgoroth.

Mount Doom remained visible at intervals as the central destination of their journey.

Gorgoroth

The plateau of Gorgoroth was one of the most hostile regions of Mordor. It was dry, ash-covered, heavily traveled by Sauron's armies, and dominated by roads connecting Barad-dûr, the Morannon, and the internal fortifications of the realm.

Frodo and Sam had little food or water and were physically exhausted by the time they began crossing it. They also had to avoid Orc patrols and at one point were forced to march with an Orc-company moving toward the north.

As they moved closer to Mount Doom, the burden of the Ring became increasingly severe for Frodo. Its physical and psychological pressure intensified as it approached the place where it had been forged and as Sauron's power surrounded them.

Sam therefore took on a greater share of the practical burden of the journey, preserving food, finding water, and supporting Frodo as his strength declined.

The Final Approach

After leaving the main roads, Frodo and Sam approached Orodruin directly. The terrain became steeper and more difficult, and Frodo eventually became too weak to continue without help.

Sam carried Frodo for part of the ascent. Although Sam had briefly borne the Ring himself at Cirith Ungol, Frodo was again carrying it and remained committed to reaching the fire.

The final road led toward the Sammath Naur, the Chambers of Fire, where Sauron had forged the One Ring. The entrance opened into the mountain above the volcanic chasm.

By this point, the quest had been reduced to the physical task of reaching the Crack of Doom before Frodo's strength and resistance to the Ring failed completely.

Gollum Returns

Gollum had followed Frodo and Sam into Mordor after surviving his earlier fall near Shelob's lair. On the slopes of Mount Doom, he attacked them in a final attempt to recover the Ring.

Sam fought him while Frodo continued upward. Although Sam had an opportunity to kill Gollum, he did not do so, in part because his own brief experience bearing the Ring had given him a greater understanding of Gollum's condition.

Gollum survived and continued following them. His presence became decisive only after Frodo reached the heart of the mountain.

The survival of Gollum therefore became one of the final contingencies upon which the destruction of the Ring depended.

The Sammath Naur

Frodo entered the Sammath Naur and reached the Crack of Doom, the place where the Ring could finally be destroyed. After carrying it across Middle-earth and resisting its influence for months, he reached the limit of his ability to surrender it voluntarily.

Instead of casting the Ring into the fire, Frodo claimed it for himself and put it on. At that moment, Sauron finally understood what his enemies had intended.

Sauron's attention turned immediately from the battle at the Black Gate toward Mount Doom. The Nazgûl were sent toward the mountain, but the realization came too late to prevent what followed.

Frodo's failure at the final moment did not end the quest because Gollum had entered the chamber behind him.

The Destruction of the One Ring

Gollum attacked the invisible Frodo and bit the Ring from his hand, severing Frodo's finger. He then recovered the object he had desired for centuries.

In his excitement, Gollum lost his footing and fell into the fire with the Ring. The One Ring was destroyed in the same fire in which Sauron had forged it more than four thousand years earlier.

Its destruction ended the power Sauron had invested in it. Because so much of his strength had been bound to the Ring, he could no longer take effective shape or dominate Middle-earth as he had before.

The central purpose of the quest was therefore achieved not because Frodo consciously completed the final act, but because the chain of earlier choices and accidents culminated in Gollum's fall.

The Fall of Sauron

The destruction of the Ring had immediate consequences across Mordor. Barad-dûr collapsed because its foundations had been made with the power of the Ring, and Sauron's will ceased to control the armies and servants gathered around him.

The Nazgûl, flying toward Mount Doom, were caught in the destruction around the mountain and perished. At the Black Gate, Sauron's armies lost the directing power that had held them together, causing panic and collapse.

Sauron himself was reduced to impotence. His spirit endured in some form, but he had permanently lost the ability to rebuild the power he had possessed.

The fate of Middle-earth therefore turned directly upon what happened inside Orodruin.

The Eruption of Mount Doom

The destruction of the Ring was followed by violent upheaval in Mordor. Mount Doom erupted, and the surrounding land shook as fire, smoke, and ash filled the air.

Frodo and Sam escaped from the Sammath Naur and descended as far as they could, but the landscape around them was collapsing. They eventually reached a place above the lava and waited, believing they would die there.

The eruption was part of the broader destruction that accompanied Sauron's fall. Roads, fortifications, and parts of the landscape around Gorgoroth were overwhelmed by fire and earthquake.

Although the Ring had been destroyed, Frodo and Sam still required rescue from the devastation that followed.

The Eagles' Rescue

Gandalf, riding with Gwaihir and other Great Eagles, entered Mordor after Sauron's fall to search for Frodo and Sam. The Eagles found the two Hobbits stranded near Mount Doom.

They carried them away before the continuing eruption could kill them. Frodo and Sam were taken to safety and later awakened in Ithilien.

Their rescue completed the physical journey that had begun in the Shire. The quest itself had ended inside Orodruin, but the survival of the Ring-bearers depended upon the collapse of Sauron's power allowing the Eagles to enter Mordor safely.

Orodruin After Sauron

After the destruction of the One Ring, Mordor ceased to function as Sauron's organized realm. Barad-dûr had fallen, the Nazgûl were destroyed, and the military system centered on Gorgoroth collapsed.

Tolkien does not give a detailed later history of Mount Doom in the Fourth Age. The mountain remained a physical feature of Mordor, but its unique political and magical importance had ended with the destruction of the Ring.

The Reunited Kingdom dealt with the peoples and territories surrounding Mordor after the war, but Orodruin itself no longer served as the forge at the center of an active Dark Lord's power.

Its greatest historical role had therefore concluded in the same place where it had begun: with the making and unmaking of the One Ring.

The Fire at the Heart of Mordor

Mount Doom's history was inseparable from Sauron's rise and fall. He established himself in Mordor, used the fires of Orodruin to forge the One Ring, and built a system of power that depended upon that Ring for much of its strength.

The failure to destroy the Ring after the Last Alliance allowed Sauron's power to survive into the Third Age. Isildur carried it away from Mordor, it remained lost for centuries, and it eventually passed through Gollum and Bilbo before reaching Frodo.

When the Ring returned to Orodruin in 3019, the mountain became the site where the central unresolved event of the Second Age was finally completed. What Isildur had declined to do after Sauron's first defeat was accomplished more than three thousand years later through Frodo's journey, Sam's endurance, and Gollum's final struggle for the Ring.

The significance of Mount Doom therefore lies in a historical circle extending across two ages. It was the place where Sauron bound much of his power into a single object and the place where that object was ultimately destroyed, bringing his dominion to an end.
