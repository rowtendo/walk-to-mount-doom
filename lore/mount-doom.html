Mount Doom

Orodruin — The Fire at the Heart of Mordor

The Mountain of Fire

In the midst of the black land of Mordor, rising alone above the ashen plain of Gorgoroth, stood a mountain of fire.

The Elves called it Orodruin.

The Men of Gondor named it Amon Amarth, the Mountain of Doom.

In later speech it became simply Mount Doom.

Smoke rose from its summit. Fire burned beneath its slopes. At times the mountain slept, and at times it awakened with violence, staining the sky above Mordor red.

Of all the places associated with Sauron, none was more closely bound to his power.

His fortress stood elsewhere.

His armies marched from many lands.

But at Orodruin he performed the deed upon which his dominion ultimately depended.

There he forged the One Ring.

And there alone could it be destroyed.

Sauron Chooses Mordor

During the Second Age, Sauron began building a new dominion in Middle-earth.

Around Second Age 1000, he chose Mordor as his stronghold.

Its geography made it formidable. Mountain ranges guarded much of its western, northern, and southern borders, while within them lay broad plains suitable for armies and industry.

But Mordor offered Sauron something else.

Orodruin.

Tolkien records that Sauron chose Mordor in part because of the mountain's fire, which he used in his sorceries and forging.

Near it he began constructing Barad-dûr, the Dark Tower.

The mountain and tower became the twin centers of his realm.

One housed his throne.

The other housed the fire in which he made his greatest weapon.

The Rings of Power

Far to the northwest of Mordor lay the Elven realm of Eregion.

There the greatest smiths of the Noldor pursued works of extraordinary skill under Celebrimbor, grandson of Fëanor.

Sauron came among them in a fair disguise.

Calling himself Annatar, the Lord of Gifts, he offered knowledge.

The Elven-smiths accepted much of what he taught.

Under his influence, Rings of Power were made.

But Sauron's purpose was domination.

While the Elves labored in Eregion, he returned secretly to Mordor.

There, in the fire of Orodruin, he began a work of his own.

The One Ring

Around Second Age 1600, Sauron entered the fire-chambers of Mount Doom.

There he forged the One Ring.

Into it he placed a great portion of his own native power so that through it he might dominate the other Rings and those who bore them.

When he put it on, however, the Elves who possessed the Three perceived him.

They understood that they had been betrayed.

They removed their Rings.

Sauron's plan for immediate mastery failed.

But the One Ring remained enormously powerful.

And because Sauron had invested so much of himself within it, the Ring became inseparably bound to his fate.

As long as it survived, the foundations of his power survived with it.

That bond would eventually destroy him.

War for the Rings

Sauron demanded that the Elves surrender the Rings of Power.

They refused.

War followed.

Sauron's armies invaded Eriador. Eregion was destroyed, and Celebrimbor was slain.

For centuries afterward, the struggle for Middle-earth continued.

Sauron rose, was humbled by Númenor, and eventually brought about the ruin of the very kingdom that had captured him.

Yet even after the Downfall of Númenor, he endured.

He returned to Mordor.

The Ring returned with him.

And the Dark Tower rose again beneath the shadow of the Mountain of Fire.

The Last Alliance

Sauron's enemies eventually united against him.

Gil-galad, High King of the Noldor, and Elendil, High King of the Dúnedain, formed the Last Alliance of Elves and Men.

Their armies marched into Mordor.

For years they besieged Barad-dûr.

At last Sauron himself came forth.

Gil-galad and Elendil fought him and were slain, though Sauron was overthrown.

Elendil's sword, Narsil, broke beneath him.

His son Isildur took up the broken blade and cut the One Ring from Sauron's hand.

The Dark Lord's bodily form was destroyed.

The war was won.

But the Ring remained.

Isildur's Choice

After Sauron's defeat, Elrond and Círdan urged Isildur to destroy the Ring.

The means to do so lay nearby.

The fires in which the Ring had been forged could unmake it.

But Isildur refused.

He claimed the Ring as compensation for the deaths of his father and brother.

And so the victors left Mordor carrying Sauron's greatest work with them.

The Ring was not destroyed.

Two years later, Isildur was ambushed by Orcs at the Gladden Fields.

The Ring slipped from his finger as he tried to escape across the Anduin.

Isildur was killed.

The Ring disappeared beneath the river.

Orodruin remained.

The Sleeping Fire

For much of the Third Age, Sauron was absent from Mordor.

Gondor guarded the approaches to the Black Land, and for centuries the Dark Lord concealed himself elsewhere.

Mount Doom grew quiet.

But Sauron had not been destroyed.

As his power returned, so too did signs of fire in Mordor.

In Third Age 2954, Mount Doom burst once more into flame.

The people of Ithilien fled before the growing terror of the region.

The mountain's awakening announced what the Wise already feared.

Sauron had returned openly to Mordor.

And the power that had forged the Ring was again active in the world.

The Road to the Fire

By then, the One Ring had been found.

It had passed from Isildur into darkness, from the Anduin to Déagol, from Déagol to Sméagol, from Sméagol to Bilbo Baggins, and finally from Bilbo to Frodo.

At the Council of Elrond, the Free Peoples confronted a terrible problem.

The Ring could not safely be used.

It could not permanently be hidden.

And no ordinary craft could destroy it.

There was only one certain way.

It had to be taken back to the place of its making.

Into Mordor.

To Mount Doom.

Across Gorgoroth

After surviving Shelob and escaping the Tower of Cirith Ungol, Frodo and Sam entered the final stage of their journey.

Before them stretched the Plateau of Gorgoroth.

Beyond it rose Mount Doom.

They were starving, exhausted, and nearly without water.

Frodo was increasingly burdened by the Ring. As he approached the place where it had been made, its weight upon him became almost unbearable.

Sam carried what he could.

At last, when Frodo could go no farther, Sam carried Frodo himself.

Behind them, meanwhile, the Captains of the West marched upon the Black Gate.

Their army could not conquer Mordor.

It was never meant to.

Aragorn and Gandalf intended to draw Sauron's attention away from the mountain.

For one final time, the fate of Middle-earth depended upon Sauron looking in the wrong direction.

The Sammath Naur

A road climbed the slopes of Orodruin to an entrance in the mountain.

Beyond it lay the Sammath Naur, the Chambers of Fire.

Sauron had made this place during the Second Age for his forging.

Within was a great fissure opening into the mountain's fire.

The Cracks of Doom.

Frodo entered.

Sam followed.

So did Gollum.

At last Frodo stood at the place where his task could be completed.

He had carried the Ring from the Shire.

He had resisted the Nazgûl.

He had survived Moria, hunger, exhaustion, terror, and the long road across Mordor.

All that remained was to let the Ring fall.

He could not do it.

The Ring Claims Frodo

At the Cracks of Doom, Frodo's resistance finally failed.

He declared that the Ring was his.

He put it on.

At that instant, Sauron understood.

His attention turned away from the battle at the Black Gate and toward Mount Doom.

For the first time, the full danger became clear to him.

The Ring was not being wielded against him.

It was inside the very mountain where it could be destroyed.

The Nazgûl raced toward Orodruin.

But another creature had already arrived.

Gollum

Gollum attacked Sam and entered the chamber.

Though Frodo was invisible, Gollum struggled with him.

Then he bit Frodo's finger from his hand.

The Ring was his again.

For one brief moment, Gollum possessed the treasure he had desired for centuries.

He danced in triumph.

And fell.

Gollum plunged into the fire.

The One Ring fell with him.

No warrior struck the final blow.

No king cast the Ring into the abyss.

The quest succeeded through a chain of choices, mercy, endurance, failure, and chance—or what, in Tolkien's moral universe, may be something more than chance.

Bilbo had once spared Gollum.

Frodo had spared him.

Sam had spared him.

And at the end, the creature repeatedly shown mercy became the means by which the Ring was destroyed when Frodo himself could no longer surrender it.

The Fall of Sauron

The effect was immediate.

The Ring was unmade.

The power Sauron had placed within it vanished from the world.

Barad-dûr collapsed.

The foundations of his realm failed.

The Nazgûl were consumed.

His armies broke.

A vast shadow rose above Mordor, terrible and powerless, before being blown away upon the wind.

Sauron was not slain in the ordinary sense, for he was one of the Maiar and could not simply cease to exist.

But he had lost so much of his native strength that he could never again take shape or dominate the peoples of Middle-earth.

His power as a Dark Lord was ended.

The mountain he had used to make the Ring had become the instrument of his ruin.

Fire and Ruin

Orodruin erupted.

Frodo and Sam fled from the Sammath Naur as the mountain tore itself apart around them.

Lava and fire spread across the land.

They reached a small hill surrounded by destruction.

There, believing themselves about to die, they waited together.

They had completed the task.

They had no strength left to escape.

But the Great Eagles, led by Gwaihir and bearing Gandalf, flew into Mordor.

Frodo and Sam were found upon the slopes and carried away.

Behind them, the old realm of Sauron collapsed in fire.

The Mountain of Doom

Mount Doom had been the furnace of Sauron's power.

There he forged the Ring through which he intended to enslave the rulers of Middle-earth.

There the victors of the Last Alliance had once possessed the opportunity to destroy it and failed.

And there, more than three thousand years later, two Hobbits reached the end of a road that armies could never have opened for them.

Yet the final moment was not a simple triumph of strength.

Frodo failed at the edge of the fire.

Gollum seized the Ring.

And the mercy shown to him long before became essential to the salvation of those who had spared him.

That is what makes Orodruin more than the place where the Ring was destroyed.

It is the place where nearly every great thread of the story meets.

Sauron's desire for control.

Isildur's refusal.

Bilbo's pity.

Frodo's endurance.

Sam's loyalty.

Gollum's obsession.

Gandalf's wisdom.

Aragorn's gamble at the Black Gate.

All of them converged upon one crack in the heart of a mountain.

Sauron had chosen Orodruin because its fire could help him create a power no enemy could overcome.

In the end, he had created the one place in Middle-earth where that power could be destroyed.

And so the fire in which the One Ring was made became the fire in which the Third Age was brought to its end.
