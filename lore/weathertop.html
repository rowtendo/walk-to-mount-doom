Amon Sûl

East of Bree, beyond the Midgewater Marshes, a solitary hill rises above the surrounding country.

Travelers in later ages called it Weathertop.

But among the Dúnedain it had another name: Amon Sûl, the Hill of Wind.

From its summit the eye could travel far across the lands of Eriador. The hill stood close to the Great East Road, commanding the country around it and forming the southernmost height of the Weather Hills.

By the end of the Third Age, little remained upon its crown but a broken ring of stone.

Once, however, a great watchtower stood there.

And within that tower was kept one of the most powerful treasures of the lost kingdom of Arnor.

The Tower of Amon Sûl

In the days of the North-kingdom, the Dúnedain built a tower upon Weathertop.

It was no ordinary watchtower.

Amon Sûl was associated with one of the seven palantíri, the Seeing-stones brought to Middle-earth by Elendil and his sons after the downfall of Númenor.

The Stone of Amon Sûl was the chief palantír of the North.

Through such stones, those with the strength and authority to use them could see distant places and communicate with other Stones. From the high tower of Amon Sûl, the Dúnedain possessed not merely a commanding view of the surrounding lands, but a link to the other centers of their realm.

For a time, Amon Sûl stood within a united kingdom.

That unity did not last.

The Breaking of Arnor

After the death of King Eärendur in Third Age 861, Arnor was divided among his three sons.

From the old North-kingdom arose Arthedain, Cardolan, and Rhudaur.

Amon Sûl stood near the meeting of their territories, and possession of its tower and palantír became a matter of bitter dispute.

Arthedain claimed the rightful kingship of all Arnor and held the other two northern palantíri. Cardolan and Rhudaur rejected that claim.

All three kingdoms desired Amon Sûl.

Thus a hill built to watch over a united realm became one of the places around which that realm's heirs quarreled.

Their division would eventually prove disastrous.

The Shadow of Angmar

Around Third Age 1300, a new power arose beyond the northeastern frontiers of the Dúnedain.

It was the realm of Angmar, ruled by a sorcerer-king whose true identity was not yet known.

He was the Witch-king, Lord of the Nazgûl.

Angmar's purpose was the destruction of the divided kingdoms of Arnor, and the Witch-king exploited their rivalries with terrible patience.

Rhudaur eventually fell under the control of evil Men allied with Angmar. Arthedain and Cardolan resisted.

Amon Sûl now stood near the front line of a war for the survival of the Dúnedain in the North.

The Fall of the Watchtower

In Third Age 1409, the armies of Angmar crossed the northern lands in overwhelming force.

They invaded Cardolan and struck against Arthedain.

The tower of Amon Sûl was attacked and destroyed.

Its defenders were defeated, but the palantír itself was saved. The Stone was carried west into Arthedain and eventually kept at Fornost alongside the Stone of Annúminas.

Cardolan was devastated, and the surviving Dúnedain of that realm were driven into diminished refuges.

The great tower upon Weathertop was never rebuilt.

Its shattered foundations remained upon the summit while the kingdoms that had fought over it continued their decline.

The End of the North-kingdom

The destruction of Amon Sûl was not the end of the war.

For centuries Arthedain survived, increasingly isolated as Angmar tightened its grip upon the North.

At last, in Third Age 1974, the Witch-king launched his final assault. Fornost fell, and Arthedain ceased to exist as a kingdom.

King Arvedui fled north and later perished at sea.

With him were lost the palantíri of Amon Sûl and Annúminas, which sank beneath the icy waters of the Bay of Forochel.

The Witch-king's triumph was brief. The following year, forces from Gondor and Lindon defeated Angmar, and its ruler fled from the North.

But the old kingdom did not return.

Its cities became ruins. Its descendants became the Rangers of the North.

And Amon Sûl remained broken beneath the open sky.

A Hill of Memory

More than a thousand years passed.

By the final centuries of the Third Age, most travelers knew little of the history of Weathertop. The Great East Road still ran beneath it, but the surrounding country had become lonely.

For the Rangers, however, the hill had not been forgotten.

The ruins belonged to the history of their people.

When Aragorn, heir of Isildur and chieftain of the Dúnedain, traveled through the wild lands of Eriador, Weathertop was part of a landscape filled with the remains of his ancestors' vanished realm.

It was toward this hill that Aragorn led four Hobbits in the autumn of 3018.

They were not the only ones seeking it.

Gandalf upon Weathertop

Only days before Frodo reached the hill, Gandalf the Grey had been there.

Having escaped imprisonment at Orthanc and learned that the Nazgûl were hunting Frodo, Gandalf rode north in pursuit.

On the night of 3 October 3018, he reached Weathertop.

There he was attacked by the Nazgûl.

A violent confrontation followed upon the summit, with flashes of fire and light visible from far away. Gandalf resisted his enemies through the night and escaped at dawn, drawing several of the Ringwraiths after him.

When Aragorn and the Hobbits later climbed Weathertop, they discovered signs that someone had recently been there.

Among them was a stone marked by Gandalf.

But Gandalf was gone.

And the Nazgûl returned.

The Knife in the Dark

On the night of 6 October, Frodo and his companions camped below the summit.

Dark shapes approached.

Five Ringwraiths entered the hollow where the travelers had taken shelter. Aragorn confronted them with fire, while Frodo drew his sword and attempted to resist.

Then Frodo put on the One Ring.

The world changed around him.

He could now perceive the Ringwraiths more clearly: not merely as cloaked figures, but as terrible remnants of the kings and sorcerers they had once been.

Their leader advanced upon him.

Frodo struck at him and cried the name of Elbereth, but the Witch-king stabbed Frodo in the shoulder with a Morgul-knife.

The blade broke within the wound.

The Nazgûl withdrew, but their attack had accomplished something more dangerous than an ordinary injury. A fragment of the weapon remained inside Frodo and began working toward his heart.

Had it reached him, Frodo would have faded and become a wraith under the dominion of the Nine.

The Road from Weathertop

Aragorn could slow the poison, but he could not heal it.

The company therefore abandoned Weathertop and hurried toward Rivendell.

Frodo weakened as they traveled.

The wound inflicted beneath Amon Sûl would trouble him long after the Ring was destroyed. Each year, as the anniversary of the attack returned, so too did some measure of its pain.

Weathertop therefore marked more than an episode on Frodo's road.

It was one of the first places where the ancient wars of Middle-earth became painfully personal to him.

The Witch-king who wounded Frodo was the same enemy who had destroyed Amon Sûl's tower nearly sixteen centuries before.

The ruins and the Ringwraith were remnants of the same long history.

The Broken Crown of the Hill

By the end of the Third Age, Weathertop was once again quiet.

Its tower was gone. Its Seeing-stone lay beneath distant northern waters. The kingdoms that had fought for possession of it had disappeared from the maps of ordinary folk.

Yet the hill had witnessed much of the history of the North.

It had stood beneath the banners of Arnor.

It had become a prize in the quarrels of Arnor's divided heirs.

It had fallen before the armies of Angmar.

Gandalf had fought the Nine upon its summit.

And beneath its ancient ruins, the Witch-king had struck the Ring-bearer with a blade forged for darker purposes.

To a traveler upon the Great East Road, Weathertop might have appeared to be only a lonely hill crowned with broken stones.

But those stones were the remains of a kingdom.

And some ruins remember more than they reveal.
