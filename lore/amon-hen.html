The Hill Above Nen Hithoel

Amon Hen, the Hill of Seeing, stood on the western side of the Great River Anduin beside the lake of Nen Hithoel. Across the water rose Amon Lhaw, the Hill of Hearing, while the steep island of Tol Brandir stood between them near the great falls of Rauros. Together these landmarks formed one of the most striking natural and historical sites in the central lands of Middle-earth.

The hills lay near the northern frontier of ancient Gondor. In the days when Gondor's power extended far up the Anduin, the region around Nen Hithoel formed part of a strategic river corridor between the southern kingdom and the lands farther north. Amon Hen and Amon Lhaw were therefore associated with Gondor's observation and defense of the river approaches.

By the end of the Third Age, the surrounding country had long since ceased to be a securely governed part of Gondor. The old works remained, but the kingdom's northern frontier had withdrawn southward over the centuries. Amon Hen consequently survived as a monument to an earlier period when Gondor had maintained a stronger presence along the upper Anduin.

The Seat of Seeing

At the summit of Amon Hen stood the Seat of Seeing, an ancient stone seat associated with the kings and rulers of Gondor. From there, a person could look out across extraordinary distances, although Tolkien never provides a mechanical explanation for how the seat worked.

The Seat of Seeing should not be confused with a palantír. There is no indication that a Seeing-stone was kept on Amon Hen, and the hill's ability appears to have belonged to the site itself or to the ancient works established there by Gondor.

The precise date when the seat was built is not recorded. Its connection with Gondor and with the paired hills of Seeing and Hearing suggests that it belonged to the period when the kingdom maintained watch over the Anduin and the northern approaches to its territory.

Gondor's Northern Watch

During the early Third Age, Gondor controlled a much larger territory than it did at the time of the War of the Ring. Its influence extended northward along the Anduin, and the river served as both a major route of travel and an important defensive line.

The region around Nen Hithoel was well suited to observation. The river narrowed and widened around steep terrain, while Rauros made further southward travel by boat impossible without leaving the water. Amon Hen's height allowed watchers to survey a broad area around the lake and the surrounding lands.

Tolkien gives relatively few details about the everyday operation of the site. There is no surviving account of a permanent garrison, a named governor, or a detailed administrative history, so Amon Hen's ancient role is best understood as part of Gondor's wider system of northern watch and defense rather than as a major fortress in its own right.

The Decline of Gondor's Northern Frontier

Gondor's power weakened over the course of the Third Age because of civil war, plague, invasions, and the loss of territory. As its population and military strength declined, many of the kingdom's more distant northern positions were abandoned.

By the later Third Age, the lands around Nen Hithoel were no longer securely controlled by Gondor. The kingdom still remembered the area as part of its historic territory, but its active defenses were concentrated much farther south.

Amon Hen remained standing after this withdrawal. Its stonework and the Seat of Seeing therefore outlasted the political system that had originally given them purpose.

The Fellowship Reaches Nen Hithoel

In February 3019, the Fellowship of the Ring traveled down the Anduin after leaving Lothlórien. Galadriel and Celeborn had provided them with boats, provisions, and Elven-cloaks, allowing the Company to make rapid progress southward.

Their journey eventually brought them to the northern end of Nen Hithoel. The Fellowship passed between the monumental figures of the Argonath, which marked the old northern boundary of Gondor, and entered the lake between the hills of Amon Hen and Amon Lhaw.

The Company camped at Parth Galen, a green lawn beneath Amon Hen on the western shore. At that point, the Fellowship had to decide whether to continue toward Gondor or turn east toward Mordor, making the ancient Gondorian site the setting for one of the most important decisions of the quest.

The Decision at Parth Galen

The Fellowship had traveled together since Rivendell, but its members did not all have the same destination. Boromir strongly favored going to Minas Tirith, where he hoped the Ring might contribute to Gondor's defense, while Frodo understood that his task ultimately required him to enter Mordor and approach Mount Doom.

Aragorn was also torn between different responsibilities. He had intended to accompany Frodo toward Mordor, but Gandalf's fall in Moria had left him responsible for leading the Company, and Boromir's concern for Gondor made the question of Minas Tirith more urgent.

At Parth Galen, Frodo asked for time alone to consider what he should do. He walked away from the camp and climbed the slopes of Amon Hen, bringing him toward the ancient Seat of Seeing.

Boromir and the Ring

Boromir followed Frodo and tried to persuade him to bring the Ring to Gondor. His argument reflected both his genuine desire to defend his people and the corrupting influence of the Ring, which encouraged him to believe that its power could be used safely against Sauron.

When Frodo refused, Boromir's desire for the Ring overcame his judgment. He attempted to take it by force, causing Frodo to put it on and disappear.

Boromir quickly recognized what he had done and was overcome with remorse. His failure did not come from simple treachery but from the same temptation that made the Ring dangerous to anyone who believed they could control it.

The incident convinced Frodo that remaining with the Fellowship placed his companions in increasing danger. He decided that he would leave alone and continue toward Mordor without asking the others to share the burden.

Frodo on the Seat of Seeing

Still wearing the Ring, Frodo reached the summit of Amon Hen and sat upon the Seat of Seeing. From there, his vision extended across vast distances beyond what ordinary sight could have reached.

He saw signs of war throughout Middle-earth, including armies, fortresses, and movements connected with Sauron's expanding power. The experience showed him that the conflict surrounding the Ring was no longer limited to the small company traveling down the Anduin but had spread across many regions at once.

Frodo's vision eventually turned toward Barad-dûr, where the Eye of Sauron began searching for him. Because Frodo was wearing the One Ring, he had become dangerously exposed to the will of its maker.

Gandalf's Intervention

At the moment when Sauron's attention was closing upon Frodo, another will opposed him. Frodo heard a voice urging him to remove the Ring, and he finally obeyed.

Tolkien later identifies the intervening power as Gandalf, who at that time had already returned to Middle-earth after his battle with the Balrog. Though physically far from Amon Hen, Gandalf was able to contest Sauron's attention long enough for Frodo to escape immediate discovery.

This episode linked Amon Hen with the wider struggle between the Wise and Sauron. The ancient Seat of Seeing gave Frodo an extraordinary view of the war, while the Ring simultaneously exposed him to the danger of being seen in return.

Frodo Chooses to Leave

After removing the Ring, Frodo made his decision. He would continue toward Mordor without the rest of the Fellowship because he feared that the Ring's influence would eventually corrupt or endanger the others.

He returned toward the boats intending to leave secretly. Sam, however, understood what Frodo was likely to do and reached the river in time to stop him from departing alone.

Frodo finally accepted Sam's presence, and the two Hobbits crossed the Anduin together. Their departure separated the Ring-bearer's journey from the remaining members of the Fellowship and began the final stage of the road toward Mordor.

The Orc Attack

While Frodo and Sam were preparing to leave, Orcs attacked the remaining members of the Fellowship. The force included Uruk-hai sent by Saruman, whose orders centered on capturing Hobbits and bringing them alive to Isengard.

The attack scattered the Company across the wooded slopes and paths around Amon Hen. Aragorn, Legolas, Gimli, Boromir, Merry, and Pippin became separated as they tried to locate Frodo and respond to the assault.

The Orcs did not understand which Hobbit carried the Ring. Their uncertainty became important because Merry and Pippin were captured while Frodo and Sam escaped across the river.

Boromir's Last Stand

Boromir encountered Merry and Pippin during the attack and defended them against the Orcs. He fought repeatedly and killed many enemies while trying to prevent the Hobbits from being captured.

Eventually he was struck by several arrows and mortally wounded. The Orcs carried Merry and Pippin away, leaving Boromir beneath the trees.

Aragorn found him before he died. Boromir confessed that he had tried to take the Ring from Frodo and expressed his regret, while Aragorn reassured him that his final defense of Merry and Pippin had restored his honor.

Boromir's death completed the personal tragedy that had begun with his temptation at Parth Galen. His actions at Amon Hen included both his greatest failure and his final act of courage.

The Breaking of the Fellowship

After the attack, Aragorn had to determine what had happened to the Company. He realized that Frodo had deliberately crossed the river and that Sam had gone with him, while Merry and Pippin had been taken westward by the Orcs.

Aragorn therefore chose not to follow Frodo. He understood that the Ring-bearer had made his decision and that attempting to pursue him would leave the captured Hobbits without help.

Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli consequently began the pursuit of the Orcs across Rohan. Their decision created the group later known as the Three Hunters, whose journey eventually brought them into the war of Rohan and the destruction of Saruman's power at Isengard.

The Fellowship as a single traveling company ended at Amon Hen, but its members continued toward different parts of the same war.

Boromir's Funeral

Before beginning the pursuit, Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli prepared Boromir's body for burial. Because they could not bury him securely on land while the Orcs were escaping, they placed him in one of the Elven boats.

They set his sword and other possessions with him and released the boat onto the waters of the Anduin. It passed over the falls of Rauros and disappeared downstream.

The funeral connected Boromir's death with the river and landscape of the ancient Gondorian frontier. A captain of Gondor died beside one of the old watch-hills of his own kingdom while defending companions whose journey had begun far beyond Gondor's borders.

Amon Hen and the War of the Ring

The events at Amon Hen changed the structure of the War of the Ring. Frodo and Sam went east toward Mordor, Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli pursued the Orcs into Rohan, and Merry and Pippin were carried toward Isengard.

Those separate journeys produced consequences far beyond the hill itself. Merry and Pippin's escape helped bring them into contact with Treebeard and the Ents, while the Three Hunters became involved in the defense of Rohan. Frodo and Sam continued toward the destruction of the Ring.

Amon Hen therefore marked a point at which one quest divided into several connected campaigns. The Fellowship ceased to travel together, but the actions of its separated members increasingly influenced the larger war against Sauron and Saruman.

The Hill of Seeing

Amon Hen had existed for centuries before the Fellowship arrived. Its ancient history belonged to the period when Gondor maintained a northern watch along the Anduin, and the Seat of Seeing remained as a surviving work of that earlier kingdom.

Tolkien records relatively little about the hill's political history between Gondor's withdrawal from the region and the War of the Ring. Unlike places such as Minas Tirith, Osgiliath, or Weathertop, Amon Hen was not the site of repeated wars or dynastic events, so its surviving importance depends chiefly on its ancient function and on what occurred there in 3019.

Those events brought several strands of the story together. Boromir's desire to use the Ring exposed its power to corrupt even a loyal defender of the Free Peoples, Frodo's experience on the Seat of Seeing revealed the scale of Sauron's war, and the Orc attack forced the Fellowship to divide.

The hill's lasting historical significance therefore comes from the decisions made there. Amon Hen began as a place from which Gondor watched the lands around the Anduin, but at the end of the Third Age it became the place where the Fellowship's common road ended and its members entered separate paths that would shape the rest of the War of the Ring.
