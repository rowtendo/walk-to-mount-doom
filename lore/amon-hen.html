The Hill Above the Anduin

Upon the western bank of the Anduin, where the Great River descended toward the lake of Nen Hithoel, rose a steep and wooded hill.

The Men of Gondor called it Amon Hen.

The Hill of Seeing.

Across the river stood its companion, Amon Lhaw, the Hill of Hearing. Between them, upon an island amid the waters, rose the rocky height of Tol Brandir.

Together these three heights stood above one of the most dramatic landscapes of the Anduin.

Northward lay the long lake of Nen Hithoel.

Southward the river narrowed and plunged through the thunderous Falls of Rauros.

And upon Amon Hen, in the final days of the Fellowship of the Ring, the fate of the quest was decided.

Yet the hill had a history long before Frodo Baggins climbed its slopes.

The Watch of Gondor

Amon Hen belonged to the ancient defenses of Gondor.

In the days of Gondor's strength, its northern frontiers extended far beyond the lands the kingdom still controlled at the end of the Third Age. The Anduin was one of the great arteries of the realm, and the heights surrounding Nen Hithoel marked an important point upon its northern approaches.

Upon Amon Hen the Gondorians built a high seat.

It became known as the Seat of Seeing.

Across the river, Amon Lhaw possessed the Seat of Hearing.

These places were associated with the watch maintained by the Men of Gondor over the lands around the Anduin.

The surrounding landscape itself proclaimed their former power.

A traveler approaching Nen Hithoel from the north first passed between the enormous Argonath: the Pillars of the Kings, carved in the likenesses of Isildur and Anárion.

Beyond those stone guardians lay the lake.

At its southern end stood Amon Hen and Amon Lhaw.

The works of Gondor had once commanded the river.

By the end of the Third Age, the kingdom had withdrawn far to the south.

The monuments remained.

The Seat of Seeing

At the summit of Amon Hen stood a stone platform reached by an ancient stair.

There was the Seat of Seeing.

Its precise powers are never fully explained.

It was not a palantír, nor does Tolkien identify it as an artifact of the same kind as the Seeing-stones. Yet from the high seat a person could perceive things far beyond the ordinary reach of sight.

How much of this ability belonged to the place itself, how much to the person sitting there, and how much depended upon other powers at work is left mysterious.

For centuries the seat stood unused while the woods reclaimed the hill around it.

Then the bearer of the One Ring came to Amon Hen.

And the Seat of Seeing awakened into the history of the War of the Ring.

The Fellowship Comes to Nen Hithoel

In February 3019, the Fellowship traveled south from Lothlórien in three Elven boats.

They followed the Anduin through increasingly dangerous country.

At last they passed beneath the Argonath.

For Aragorn, the moment carried special meaning. He was the heir of Isildur, returning beneath the image of his ancestor into lands once ruled by his people.

Beyond the pillars, the Fellowship entered Nen Hithoel.

They made camp at Parth Galen, a green lawn upon the western shore beneath Amon Hen.

There they could delay their greatest decision no longer.

Their road had reached a dividing point.

To the west lay Minas Tirith.

To the east lay Mordor.

The Choice of the Ring-bearer

The Fellowship had never agreed that every member would accompany Frodo all the way to Mount Doom.

Boromir intended to return to Minas Tirith, where Gondor was preparing for war. Aragorn had long expected to go there as well, though Gandalf's fall in Moria had forced him to assume leadership of the Company.

Frodo now had to decide where he would go.

And he asked for time alone.

He walked into the woods beneath Amon Hen to consider his choice.

Boromir followed him.

Boromir's Temptation

Boromir was a brave man.

He was also desperate.

For years Gondor had borne the weight of Sauron's growing power. Its soldiers defended the crossings of the Anduin while Mordor gathered strength beyond the mountains.

To Boromir, the Ring appeared not merely as a temptation but as a possible weapon.

Why should it be destroyed when it might be used against the Enemy?

He tried to persuade Frodo to take it to Minas Tirith.

Frodo refused.

Boromir pressed harder.

At last, overwhelmed by the Ring's temptation, he attempted to seize it.

Frodo put on the Ring and vanished.

Almost immediately, Boromir understood what he had done.

His anger passed.

He called after Frodo, begging forgiveness.

But Frodo was already climbing Amon Hen.

Upon the High Seat

Still wearing the Ring, Frodo reached the summit and sat upon the Seat of Seeing.

Then his sight was carried far beyond the hill.

He saw lands and armies across Middle-earth.

War gathered everywhere.

He glimpsed the Misty Mountains, Mirkwood, the lands of the Rohirrim, ships upon distant waters, and the strength of Men preparing for battle.

At last his vision turned toward Barad-dûr.

There, in Mordor, the Eye of Sauron began searching for him.

Frodo felt the terrible pressure of Sauron's will.

Then another voice reached him.

Take it off!

Frodo obeyed.

He removed the Ring.

The Eye passed him by.

Only later is it made clear that the voice had been Gandalf, returned from death and striving against Sauron's attention from afar.

For a brief moment upon Amon Hen, the Ring-bearer had stood between two great powers.

Then he was alone again.

And his choice had been made.

Frodo's Decision

What happened with Boromir convinced Frodo of something he had increasingly feared.

The Ring would endanger anyone who remained near him.

Even a good and valiant companion could be corrupted by it.

Frodo therefore decided to continue to Mordor alone.

He descended from Amon Hen and returned secretly to the boats.

But Samwise Gamgee understood him too well.

Sam realized what Frodo intended and ran after him. Frodo attempted to leave by boat, but Sam followed into the river despite being unable to swim.

Frodo pulled him aboard.

The two Hobbits crossed the Anduin together.

The quest to destroy the Ring continued eastward.

Behind them, the Fellowship was falling apart.

The Orc Attack

While Frodo and Sam escaped, enemies descended upon the others.

Companies of Orcs attacked around Amon Hen and Parth Galen.

Among them were the Uruk-hai of Isengard, sent by Saruman with orders to capture Hobbits and bring them back alive.

The Fellowship became scattered in the woods.

Merry and Pippin were found by Boromir.

And Boromir made his final choice.

The Last Stand of Boromir

Only a short time earlier, Boromir had tried to take the Ring from Frodo.

Now he stood between Merry and Pippin and an army of Orcs.

He sounded the Great Horn of Gondor.

Its call echoed through the woods.

Boromir fought until many Orcs lay dead around him. But enemies continued to come.

Arrows struck him.

Still he fought.

At last he fell beneath a tree, pierced by many wounds.

The Orcs seized Merry and Pippin and fled.

Aragorn arrived too late to save him.

Boromir confessed that he had tried to take the Ring from Frodo. He told Aragorn that the Orcs had captured the two younger Hobbits.

Then Boromir died.

Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli placed his body in one of the Elven boats with his sword, shield, and the weapons of his fallen enemies.

They sent him over the Falls of Rauros.

Thus the son of the Steward of Gondor passed from the Fellowship.

The Breaking of the Fellowship

By the end of that day, the Company that had departed Rivendell was no more.

Gandalf had fallen in Moria, though unknown to his companions he had returned.

Boromir was dead.

Merry and Pippin were prisoners.

Frodo and Sam had crossed the river toward Mordor.

Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli remained upon the western bank.

Aragorn faced a choice of his own.

He could attempt to follow Frodo.

Or he could pursue the Orcs who had taken Merry and Pippin.

He chose the captives.

The Three Hunters began their pursuit across Rohan.

From that decision would come the meeting with Éomer, the return of Gandalf, the defense of Rohan, the Battle of Helm's Deep, and eventually Aragorn's road toward the throne.

The Fellowship had broken.

Its purpose had not.

A Hill Between Roads

No great city stood upon Amon Hen.

No people made their permanent home beneath its trees.

Its age of importance as a Gondorian watch-post had already passed long before the War of the Ring.

Yet few places played a more decisive role in the journey of the Fellowship.

At Amon Hen, every road divided.

Boromir chose between possession and repentance.

Frodo chose between companionship and the burden he believed he must carry alone.

Sam chose Frodo over safety.

Aragorn chose to rescue the captured rather than pursue the Ring.

Merry and Pippin were carried toward a chain of events that would awaken the Ents and help overthrow Saruman.

And the Fellowship ceased to be nine companions traveling upon a single road.

From Amon Hen they scattered across Middle-earth.

Yet in scattering, they carried the war into places the Enemy had not foreseen.

The hill had once been built for watching.

At the end of the Third Age, it became a place of choosing.

And from the choices made beneath its trees, the course of the War of the Ring was changed.
