The Ancient Settlement

Bree was an old settlement in central Eriador, standing where the Great East Road passed near the remains of the ancient north-south road later called the Greenway. It lay about forty miles east of the Shire and roughly midway between the Shire and Weathertop, making it an important stopping place for travelers crossing the otherwise sparsely populated lands of western Middle-earth.

The name Bree applied both to the principal village and, more broadly, to the small inhabited region surrounding Bree-hill. Four villages made up the Bree-land: Bree itself stood against the western side of the hill, Staddle lay on its southeastern slopes, Combe occupied a valley to the east, and Archet stood farther north among the trees of the Chetwood. Together they formed one of the few substantial communities that survived in central Eriador by the end of the Third Age.

Bree was particularly unusual because Men and Hobbits lived there together. Tolkien describes this arrangement as unique within Middle-earth, and by the time of the War of the Ring it had existed for many generations. The Big Folk and Little Folk maintained their own households and customs while sharing the same villages, roads, markets, and local concerns.

The Men of Bree

The Men of Bree considered themselves descendants of people who had inhabited the region long before the establishment of the kingdoms of the Dúnedain. Their own traditions held that their ancestors had lived in the lands around Bree before the Númenóreans returned to Middle-earth and founded Arnor at the end of the Second Age.

Tolkien does not provide a detailed account of Bree's earliest centuries, so the precise origin of its population cannot be reconstructed with certainty. The settlement's importance lies partly in this continuity: while kingdoms rose and disappeared around it, Bree remained an inhabited community whose people were primarily concerned with their farms, trades, families, and local affairs.

This made Bree quite different from nearby places such as Annúminas, Fornost, and Weathertop. Those sites became ruins after the fall of the North-kingdom, whereas Bree survived as an ordinary inhabited settlement. By the late Third Age, its residents lived among the remnants of a political world far older and larger than their own community, though most knew relatively little about that history.

The Hobbits of Bree

Hobbits were also established in Bree long before the events of The Lord of the Rings. Their presence there reached back to the period when Hobbit peoples were migrating westward across Eriador from lands east of the Misty Mountains. Bree consequently became an important stage in the westward movement that eventually led to the settlement of the Shire.

In Third Age 1601, the brothers Marcho and Blanco, leaders among the Fallohide Hobbits, received permission from King Argeleb II of Arthedain to cross the Baranduin and settle the lands beyond it. Tolkien's Prologue states that these Hobbits came from Bree, and many others followed them westward. Their settlement became the Shire, whose own calendar later counted 1601 as the first year of Shire-reckoning.

Not every Hobbit left Bree for the new country. A permanent Hobbit population remained in Bree-land, especially in Staddle, and continued to live alongside Men throughout the Third Age. The Hobbits of Bree therefore preserved a connection with an earlier stage of Hobbit history that predated the establishment of the Shire itself.

Bree and the North-kingdom

When Bree's recorded history becomes clearer, the surrounding lands formed part of Arnor, the northern kingdom founded by Elendil and his sons after the downfall of Númenor. Arnor originally extended across much of Eriador, and the roads passing through Bree connected important regions of the Dúnedain realm.

After the death of King Eärendur in Third Age 861, Arnor was divided among his sons into Arthedain, Cardolan, and Rhudaur. Bree lay within the lands associated with Arthedain, close enough to the disputed central regions of Eriador that the wars among the successor kingdoms and the later rise of Angmar affected the wider country around it.

The importance of the nearby roads increased because they connected Bree with places such as Fornost, Weathertop, Rivendell, and the lands farther south. The Great East Road had much older origins and continued west toward the Shire and east toward the Misty Mountains, while the north-south road linked the northern Dúnedain lands with the route toward Tharbad and Gondor. Bree stood near the meeting of these routes and benefited from the traffic that survived along them.

The Wars with Angmar

Around Third Age 1300, the kingdom of Angmar arose in the north under the Witch-king, whose purpose was to destroy the remaining Dúnedain kingdoms of Arnor. For several centuries, warfare spread across northern Eriador as Angmar exploited the divisions among Arthedain, Cardolan, and Rhudaur.

In 1409, Angmar launched a major invasion across the region. The fortress of Amon Sûl on Weathertop was destroyed, King Arveleg I of Arthedain was killed, and Cardolan was devastated. Although Tolkien does not record a specific attack upon Bree during these wars, the settlement lay within the broader region affected by the collapse of Dúnedain power and the increasing insecurity of the surrounding countryside.

The struggle continued until 1974, when Angmar captured Fornost and Arthedain finally fell. The Witch-king was defeated the following year by forces from Gondor and Lindon at the Battle of Fornost, but the northern kingdom was not re-established. Bree consequently survived the destruction of the political order that had once governed the roads and lands around it.

Bree After the Fall of Arnor

After the fall of Arthedain, much of Eriador became increasingly depopulated. Annúminas and Fornost were abandoned, many settlements disappeared, and the great roads passed through long stretches of empty country. Bree and the Shire were among the notable exceptions, remaining inhabited while much of the former North-kingdom declined into wilderness.

The descendants of the northern Dúnedain survived as the Rangers of the North, who continued to patrol Eriador without a kingdom of their own. They guarded roads and settlements against Orcs, wolves, trolls, and other dangers, but they did so without openly ruling the people they protected. Most inhabitants of Bree therefore knew the Rangers as mysterious and sometimes unwelcome wanderers rather than as the heirs of the kings who had once governed the region.

This difference between appearance and reality became an important feature of Bree's later history. Its people regarded themselves as living largely on their own in an empty country, while the Rangers quietly helped make that independence possible. Aragorn, who was Chieftain of the Dúnedain during the final decades of the Third Age, was known in Bree simply as Strider.

The Crossroads of Eriador

Despite the decline of the surrounding lands, Bree continued to benefit from its position on the Great East Road. Travelers still passed between the Shire, Rivendell, the Misty Mountains, and more distant regions, while the old Greenway brought occasional traffic from the south. Bree therefore retained a degree of contact with the wider world that was unusual among the surviving settlements of Eriador.

The village itself was protected by a deep ditch and thick hedge, with gates where the road entered and left the settlement. Gatekeepers controlled access after nightfall, reflecting the practical caution of a community surrounded by sparsely inhabited country. Houses of Men stood mainly upon the lower slopes of Bree-hill, while many Hobbits lived in holes constructed in the hillside or in houses suited to their size.

Because travelers needed food, shelter, news, and stabling, Bree also developed a long tradition of hospitality. At the center of that tradition stood an inn that would become the most famous institution in Bree-land.

The Prancing Pony

The Prancing Pony was an old inn near the center of Bree, kept at the end of the Third Age by Barliman Butterbur. It served both Men and Hobbits and contained rooms suited to guests of different sizes. Its common room brought together local residents, merchants, travelers, Dwarves, and other strangers passing along the road.

The inn's importance extended beyond providing lodging. In a region where settlements were separated by large distances, it functioned as a place where news from different parts of Eriador could be exchanged. Travelers arriving from the Shire, the east, or the southern road carried stories that might otherwise never have reached the people of Bree.

The Prancing Pony was also one of the places where Gandalf and Aragorn were known. Butterbur served as an intermediary between them on at least one crucial occasion, though his failure to deliver a letter from Gandalf to Frodo in the summer of 3018 nearly had serious consequences for the Ring-bearer's journey.

The Approach of the War of the Ring

In June 3018, Gandalf passed through Bree and left a letter with Butterbur for Frodo Baggins. The letter warned Frodo to leave the Shire earlier than originally planned and told him that he could trust Aragorn if they met. Butterbur forgot to arrange for the letter's delivery, so Frodo remained unaware of Gandalf's warning until he eventually reached Bree himself.

By the autumn of 3018, the search for the One Ring had brought Sauron's servants into Eriador. The Nazgûl crossed the region searching for a Hobbit named Baggins and reached Bree shortly after Frodo and his companions entered the village. Bree, which had remained largely outside the great wars of Middle-earth for centuries, suddenly became part of the central conflict of the age.

Frodo, Sam, Merry, and Pippin arrived at the Prancing Pony on 29 September 3018, using the name Underhill in an attempt to conceal Frodo's identity. Their efforts at secrecy were weakened when Frodo accidentally put on the One Ring in the common room and disappeared in front of numerous witnesses. The incident attracted attention at precisely the moment when secrecy was most important.

Strider and the Hobbits

At the Prancing Pony, the Hobbits encountered Aragorn, whom the people of Bree knew as Strider. His worn clothing and reputation as a Ranger initially made Frodo suspicious, but Butterbur eventually remembered Gandalf's letter, which confirmed that Aragorn was the man Gandalf had instructed Frodo to trust.

Aragorn warned the Hobbits that the Black Riders were searching for them and advised them not to sleep in their assigned rooms. That precaution proved important when attackers entered the inn during the night and slashed the beds where they expected the Hobbits to be sleeping. Tolkien leaves some uncertainty concerning the precise identity of the human agents involved in the attack, but the Nazgûl were operating in Bree-land and had already gained information from people willing to assist them.

The following morning, Aragorn led the Hobbits out of Bree by routes intended to avoid the Black Riders. Their departure began the journey through the wilderness toward Weathertop and Rivendell, while Bree was left to deal with the fear and uncertainty caused by the appearance of the Riders.

Bree During the War

As the War of the Ring developed, the Rangers of the North were drawn away from their usual patrols. Many of them rode south with Halbarad in the Grey Company to join Aragorn, reducing the protection that Bree and the surrounding country had long received without fully understanding it.

The consequences became visible during the months that followed. When Gandalf and the Hobbits returned to Bree after the defeat of Sauron, Butterbur told them that the region had experienced serious trouble. Unfamiliar Men had come north, local people had been attacked, and several inhabitants of Bree had been killed. The village had strengthened its defenses and become far more suspicious of outsiders.

These events revealed how much Bree had depended upon the Rangers. The people had often regarded them as disreputable wanderers, but once the Rangers were absent, dangers that had previously been kept away reached the settlement itself. The same pattern occurred in the Shire, where the withdrawal of the Rangers contributed to the conditions that allowed Saruman's Men to take control.

The Return of the King

After Sauron's defeat, Aragorn became King Elessar, restoring the kingship of the Dúnedain in both Gondor and the north. The former territories of Arnor became part of the Reunited Kingdom, bringing Bree once again within a larger political realm after more than a thousand years without a northern king.

For the people of Bree, this restoration had an unusual personal dimension. The mysterious Ranger they had known for years as Strider was now the king whose authority extended over their lands. Butterbur was astonished when Gandalf explained that Aragorn had become king, since the Bree-folk had never understood the true identity or ancestry of the Rangers who traveled through their country.

The restoration of the North-kingdom also promised renewed security for the roads and settlements of Eriador. Aragorn later re-established Annúminas as a northern royal residence, and the return of organized government allowed the old lands of Arnor to begin recovering after centuries of depopulation and danger.

Bree in the Fourth Age

Bree continued as an inhabited community into the Fourth Age. Unlike many ancient sites of Eriador, it did not need to be rebuilt or recolonized because its people had survived continuously through the decline of Arnor, the wars with Angmar, and the disturbances surrounding the War of the Ring.

Its relationship with the restored kingdom was therefore one of continuity rather than replacement. Bree remained a settlement of Men and Hobbits with its own customs and local identity, but it now existed within the peaceful northern realm of King Elessar. The Rangers who had once protected the country secretly were no longer a wandering remnant of a fallen kingdom; their chieftain had become king and the political order they represented had been restored.

The roads around Bree also gained new significance. The Great East Road once again connected inhabited and friendly lands, while the old north-south route belonged to a kingdom that united the northern and southern Dúnedain under one ruler. Bree's position at the crossroads, which had allowed it to survive through centuries of decline, placed it naturally within the renewed communications of the Fourth Age.

The Survival of Bree

Bree's history was very different from that of the great kingdoms and fortresses surrounding it. It possessed no famous royal dynasty, no great army, and no monumental works comparable to Annúminas, Fornost, or Weathertop. Its historical importance came instead from continuity: while those greater places fell into ruin, Bree remained inhabited.

The settlement survived the division and destruction of Arnor, the wars with Angmar, and the long depopulation of Eriador. Men and Hobbits continued to live together around Bree-hill, travelers continued to use the Great East Road, and the Prancing Pony remained a meeting place in a region where such places had become rare. This persistence made Bree one of the principal surviving links between the populated Shire and the wider lands east of it.

Bree also occupied an important position in the history of the War of the Ring. It was there that Frodo first met Aragorn, where Gandalf's delayed warning finally reached him, and where the Hobbits came into direct contact with the widening search of the Nazgûl. The events at the Prancing Pony transformed what had begun as a journey out of the Shire into a flight through lands where Sauron's servants were already searching for the Ring.

By the beginning of the Fourth Age, Bree had outlasted the political world in which it had first developed and witnessed the restoration of that world in a new form. The kings of Arnor had vanished, their roads had become lonely, and their descendants had wandered the wilderness as Rangers; centuries later, the last Chieftain of those Rangers restored the northern kingdom. Through all of those changes, Bree remained at the crossroads.
