An Ancient Settlement

East of the Shire, where the Great East Road met the Greenway, there stood a cluster of settlements older than the realm of the Hobbits.

This was the Bree-land.

Its chief village, Bree, lay against the western slopes of Bree-hill, a wooded rise standing alone amid the country of central Eriador. Around it were three smaller communities: Staddle, Combe, and Archet.

The Men of Bree claimed that they were descended from the first Men to settle in western Middle-earth and that their people had inhabited the region from ancient days. Whatever the full truth of that tradition, Bree was unquestionably old. Kingdoms had risen and vanished around it, roads had fallen into ruin, and wilderness had swallowed lands once populated by Men.

Bree endured.

The Coming of the Hobbits

The Bree-land was remarkable for another reason.

Here Men and Hobbits lived side by side.

Long before the Shire was founded, Hobbits migrating westward had reached Bree. Some remained there permanently, while others continued onward. Indeed, it was from the country around Bree that Marcho and Blanco later led their followers west across the Brandywine and founded the Shire in Third Age 1601.

Those who stayed became the Hobbits of Bree.

They lived principally at Staddle, though Hobbit-holes could also be found in Bree itself. The relationship between the Big Folk and Little Folk was an old and comfortable one. They shared roads, news, surnames, and inns while keeping houses suited to their respective sizes.

Tolkien describes Bree as the only place in Middle-earth where the two peoples lived together in such close and lasting friendship.

Four Villages Beneath Bree-hill

Though travelers often spoke simply of "Bree," the Bree-land consisted of four villages.

Bree itself was the largest, built upon the western side of Bree-hill. A hedge and ditch enclosed the settlement, and gates stood where the Great East Road entered and departed.

On the southeastern side of the hill lay Staddle, whose population consisted chiefly of Hobbits.

Farther east, in a deep valley beneath the northern slopes, stood Combe.

Beyond it, among the trees near the edge of the Chetwood, was Archet, the most secluded of the four.

Together they formed a small island of settled country surrounded by lands that had become increasingly empty and wild.

The Roads of a Lost Kingdom

Bree owed much of its importance to its position.

The Great East Road ran through it from the direction of the Shire toward Rivendell and the Misty Mountains. Crossing it at Bree was an older north-south road. In later days this became known as the Greenway, for grass had grown over a road once busy with travelers.

That fading highway preserved the memory of a different world.

In the days of the North-kingdom, roads around Bree had carried soldiers, merchants, messengers, and travelers through populated lands. The Greenway ran north toward Fornost, the royal city of Arthedain, and south toward the lands of Gondor.

But the northern kingdoms declined. War with Angmar, plague, and the long years of the Third Age emptied much of Eriador. After the fall of Arthedain in 1974, the country surrounding Bree became increasingly lonely.

The roads remained.

Most of the travelers did not.

And so Bree became something unusual: an inhabited crossroads standing amid the ruins and wilderness of a much older civilization.

The Prancing Pony

At the heart of Bree stood its most famous establishment: The Prancing Pony.

For generations the inn was kept by the Butterbur family, and in the final years of the Third Age its proprietor was Barliman Butterbur.

The Pony served both Men and Hobbits, with rooms specially suited to the Little Folk. Travelers from distant regions passed beneath its roof, and news from the outside world often reached Bree through its common room long before it reached the quiet settlements farther west.

Dwarves journeying along the East Road were familiar guests. Men came north and south. Bree-folk gathered for ale and conversation. Even the occasional traveler from the Shire might appear.

And sometimes there were Rangers.

The Rangers of the North

To most inhabitants of Bree, the Rangers were strange wanderers.

They wore weather-stained clothing, traveled alone or in small companies, and spoke little of themselves. The Bree-folk knew them as people of the wilderness and generally regarded them with suspicion.

They did not know that these wanderers were the Dúnedain of the North, descendants of the Men of ancient Arnor.

Nor did they understand how much they owed them.

While the villages slept behind their gates, the Rangers watched the empty lands of Eriador. They guarded roads and settlements against dangers that most Bree-landers scarcely knew existed.

Among these Rangers was a grim traveler known in Bree simply as Strider.

His true name was Aragorn, son of Arathorn.

And he was the heir of the kings whose realm had once surrounded Bree.

Four Hobbits and a Ranger

In the autumn of 3018, Bree became unexpectedly entangled in the fate of the One Ring.

Four Hobbits arrived at the Prancing Pony: Frodo Baggins, Samwise Gamgee, Meriadoc Brandybuck, and Peregrin Took.

Frodo traveled under the name Mr. Underhill, hoping to pass unnoticed.

The attempt did not go particularly well.

During an evening in the common room, Frodo accidentally put on the One Ring and vanished before the astonished patrons. The incident immediately made the strangers the chief subject of local gossip.

More dangerous eyes were watching as well.

The Nazgûl were searching for Frodo, and agents of the Enemy had reached Bree before him. The Hobbits' ponies were driven away, their intended beds were attacked during the night, and their road east had become perilous.

Their unlikely protector was Strider.

At first Frodo mistrusted him. A forgotten letter from Gandalf, preserved by an embarrassed Barliman Butterbur, finally helped establish the Ranger's identity and intentions.

The following morning the Hobbits left Bree under Aragorn's guidance.

Without that meeting in the Prancing Pony, the history of the War of the Ring might have ended very differently.

Trouble on the Roads

The departure of the Ring-bearer did not mean that Bree escaped the growing disorder.

As Sauron's power increased and the Rangers were drawn away, lawless Men began moving north along the Greenway. The lands around Bree became dangerous, and the inhabitants were forced to defend themselves.

By the time Gandalf and the four Hobbits returned after the fall of Sauron, the gates of Bree were shut and guarded after nightfall.

There had been fighting.

Several Bree-landers had been killed, and fear of outsiders had grown. Barliman Butterbur complained bitterly of the disappearance of the Rangers, not yet realizing that the mysterious Strider he had known was now King Elessar.

The world beyond Bree had changed.

So too had the travelers returning through its gates.

The King Returns

With Sauron's defeat, the ancient kingdoms of the Dúnedain were reunited under Aragorn Elessar.

For Bree this had special significance.

The forgotten roads were once again roads of a kingdom. The wandering Ranger who had sat quietly in the Prancing Pony was now the sovereign of the restored northern lands.

Gandalf assured Butterbur that better days lay ahead. Travelers would return. The roads would become safer. The wilderness that had pressed upon Bree for centuries would no longer be quite so threatening.

Yet Bree itself required little transformation.

Its strength had always been its ability to endure.

The Crossroads Endure

Bree was never a great city.

No famous king built a palace there. No Elven-lord made it a hidden refuge. No great battle determined the fate of an age beneath Bree-hill.

Its importance was quieter than that.

For centuries it remained a lamp beside an old road: a place where travelers could find food, ale, news, a bed, and perhaps more company than they had intended.

Men and Hobbits lived there together while ancient kingdoms disappeared around them. Rangers passed unnoticed through its gates. Dwarves stopped on their long journeys east and west. And in the common room of an old inn, four frightened Hobbits once met the man who would become King.

The great roads of Eriador crossed at Bree.

For that reason, sooner or later, so did many of its stories.
