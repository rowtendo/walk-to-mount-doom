Bree-land

East of the Shire and west of the Weather Hills lay Bree-land, a small inhabited region centered upon Bree-hill. By the end of the Third Age, it was one of the few places in central Eriador where a settled population of Men remained.

Bree-land contained four villages: Bree, Staddle, Combe, and Archet. Bree itself stood against the western side of Bree-hill, while Staddle lay on its southeastern slopes. Combe was situated in a valley east of the hill, and Archet lay farther north near the edge of the Chetwood.

The region was remarkable for another reason. Men and Hobbits lived there together, a situation Tolkien describes as unique. The two peoples maintained their own households and customs but had shared Bree-land peacefully for many generations.

Despite its small size in Frodo's time, Bree was very old. Its history reached back well before the founding of the Shire.

An Ancient Settlement

The Men of Bree claimed that they were the original inhabitants of the region and that their ancestors had lived there before the return of the Númenóreans to Middle-earth. Tolkien associates them with peoples who had once inhabited parts of western Middle-earth during the Elder Days and who had survived the great upheavals of earlier ages.

Bree therefore predated the northern kingdom of Arnor, which Elendil founded at the end of the Second Age. When the Dúnedain established their realm across Eriador, Bree and the surrounding country became part of a much larger and more populous political landscape.

Tolkien does not provide a detailed account of Bree during the early centuries of Arnor, and there is no known line of kings or lords specifically ruling Bree-land. Its importance appears to have come primarily from its location. Two major roads met near Bree, making the settlement a natural stopping place for travelers moving through Eriador.

The Crossroads

The Great East Road ran through Bree-land from west to east. In earlier ages it connected the lands around the Grey Havens and the western regions of Eriador with Rivendell and the passes of the Misty Mountains. The road continued to be used in the Third Age, though traffic diminished as the population of Eriador declined.

A second road crossed it near Bree. In Frodo's time this was generally called the Greenway, but it had once been the great North-South Road connecting the northern kingdom of Arnor with Gondor in the south. Northward it led toward Fornost; southward it passed through the lands that later became Rohan and continued toward Gondor.

Bree consequently stood near one of the most important road junctions in the northern lands. In the days when Arnor was strong, these roads linked kingdoms and cities. By the late Third Age, the Greenway was grass-grown and much less traveled, reflecting the decline of the civilization that had built and maintained it.

Bree nevertheless endured beside the crossroads long after many larger settlements disappeared.

The Coming of the Hobbits

The history of Bree was also closely connected with the westward migration of the Hobbits.

Hobbits had originally lived farther east, particularly in and around the upper valleys of the Anduin. During the early Third Age, groups of Harfoots, Stoors, and Fallohides gradually crossed the Misty Mountains and entered Eriador.

Some settled around Bree.

The Hobbit population of Bree-land therefore existed before the Shire itself. When Marcho and Blanco, two Fallohide brothers, crossed the Brandywine in Third Age 1601 and founded the Shire with the permission of King Argeleb II of Arthedain, they came westward from the region of Bree.

Not every Hobbit followed them. A population remained in Bree-land, especially at Staddle, and continued living alongside the Big Folk.

This gave Bree an unusual place in Hobbit history. It was both a permanent Hobbit settlement in its own right and one of the last major stopping places between the Shire and the wider world.

Bree and the North-kingdom

Bree existed within the lands of the northern Dúnedain during the long decline of Arnor. After Arnor was divided in Third Age 861, the area of Bree fell within Arthedain, the northwestern successor kingdom ruled by the senior line of Isildur's descendants.

Farther east, the kingdoms of Cardolan and Rhudaur declined during the wars with Angmar. Arthedain survived longer, but it too was eventually destroyed. In Third Age 1974, the Witch-king's forces captured Fornost and brought the North-kingdom to an end.

The following year, Angmar was defeated at the Battle of Fornost, but Arthedain was not immediately restored. Its surviving Dúnedain became a wandering people, and the line of kings continued through the Chieftains of the Dúnedain.

Bree survived these upheavals.

Tolkien does not describe the village as a battlefield during the wars with Angmar, nor does he give a detailed account of how those wars affected its inhabitants. What is clear is that Bree remained inhabited while much of the surrounding region became increasingly empty.

A Settlement in a Depopulated Land

By the end of the Third Age, Eriador was far less populous than it had been during the height of Arnor. Cities such as Annúminas and Fornost had fallen into ruin, while stretches of the old roads passed through wilderness.

Bree-land was one of the exceptions.

The four villages continued to support farmers, innkeepers, craftsmen, and other ordinary inhabitants. Bree itself was protected by a deep ditch and thick hedge, with gates controlling the road where it entered and left the village. These defenses were modest compared with the fortresses of the old Dúnedain, but they were suited to a small rural community.

Its position on the roads also meant that Bree remained more connected with outsiders than the Shire. Travelers, Dwarves, merchants, and other wanderers still occasionally passed through.

Not all of those travelers were understood by the Bree-folk.

The Rangers of the North

Among the regular visitors to Bree were the Rangers.

The Bree-folk generally regarded them as mysterious and somewhat disreputable wanderers. They knew little about the Rangers' ancestry or purpose, and even less about the role they played in protecting the surviving settlements of Eriador.

In reality, the Rangers were the remnants of the Dúnedain of Arnor, descended from the people who had once ruled much of the North. Their Chieftain at the end of the Third Age was Aragorn II, the heir of Isildur.

Aragorn was known in Bree simply as Strider.

The contrast was considerable. To many inhabitants of Bree he appeared to be an unkempt traveler of uncertain business. In fact, he was the direct descendant of the kings under whose authority Bree-land had once existed.

The Rangers helped guard the lands around Bree and the Shire, though the people benefiting from that protection seldom realized its extent.

The Prancing Pony

At the center of Bree's role as a crossroads stood The Prancing Pony.

The inn was kept in Frodo's time by Barliman Butterbur, whose family had managed it for generations. Because both Men and Hobbits visited the inn, it contained rooms suited to guests of different sizes.

The Pony served as more than a place to eat and sleep. In a region without rapid communication, an inn at a major crossroads became a center for news. Local inhabitants gathered there, while travelers brought stories from distant lands.

Dwarves journeying between the Blue Mountains and lands farther east sometimes passed through Bree. Men from the surrounding country came to the inn, as did Hobbits from the Shire and Bree-land. Rangers also appeared there, though they were not always warmly received.

This mixture made the Prancing Pony one of the few places in northern Eriador where the sheltered world of the Hobbits regularly intersected with events beyond their borders.

In Third Age 3018, that intersection became particularly important.

Frodo Comes to Bree

On 29 September 3018, Frodo Baggins, Sam Gamgee, Merry Brandybuck, and Pippin Took arrived in Bree while traveling toward Rivendell.

Frodo was using the name Mr. Underhill in an attempt to conceal his identity. The Hobbits intended to meet Gandalf at the Prancing Pony, but Gandalf was not there.

Their arrival attracted considerable attention. Four Hobbits from the Shire traveling east together were unusual enough, and Frodo accidentally made himself even more conspicuous in the common room when he fell and the One Ring slipped onto his finger, causing him to disappear in front of the guests.

Among those watching was Strider.

Aragorn knew that the Hobbits were in serious danger. The Nazgûl were searching for Frodo, and agents connected with the Enemy had already reached Bree.

Gandalf's Letter

Aragorn's attempt to gain Frodo's trust was complicated by an earlier mistake.

Gandalf had passed through Bree months before and left a letter with Butterbur for Frodo. The letter warned Frodo to leave the Shire quickly and explained that he could trust a Ranger called Strider.

Butterbur had forgotten to send it.

When he finally remembered and gave the letter to Frodo, Gandalf's words helped confirm Aragorn's identity and intentions. The letter also contained a version of the poem associated with Aragorn, including the line "Not all those who wander are lost."

Frodo accepted Aragorn as their guide.

That decision was crucial. Gandalf was absent, the Nazgûl were nearby, and the Hobbits had little knowledge of the wilderness between Bree and Rivendell. Aragorn knew both the land and the enemy pursuing them.

The Nazgûl in Bree

The danger became clear that night.

The Hobbits did not sleep in the rooms originally assigned to them. During the night, attackers entered those rooms and slashed the beds, apparently believing the travelers were inside.

At the same time, the ponies and horses in the inn's stables were driven away.

The precise identities of all those involved in the attack are not entirely explicit in Tolkien's narrative, though the Nazgûl and their local agents were operating in the area. What mattered immediately was that the Hobbits could no longer safely remain in Bree.

The following morning, they acquired a poorly treated pony from Bill Ferny, a local man who had dealings with suspicious strangers. Sam later named the animal Bill.

With Aragorn as their guide, the Hobbits left Bree and avoided the Great East Road as much as possible. Their next major destination was Weathertop.

Bree During the War of the Ring

The departure of Aragorn and the movement of the Rangers southward had consequences for Bree-land.

As the War of the Ring developed, lawlessness increased in Eriador. Outsiders came north, and Bree experienced disturbances more serious than anything its inhabitants had known for many years.

When Gandalf and the Hobbits returned to Bree after the fall of Sauron, Barliman Butterbur told them that there had been fighting in the village and that several people had been killed. The gates had been strengthened and the inhabitants had become more suspicious of strangers.

The trouble in Bree was part of a wider breakdown of order in the North. Similar groups of lawless Men had entered the Shire, where Saruman eventually established control through his ruffians.

Bree survived, but the events demonstrated how much the region had unknowingly depended upon the Rangers.

The Return of the King

The defeat of Sauron in Third Age 3019 transformed Bree's political position.

Strider, the obscure Ranger whom many Bree-folk had regarded with suspicion, was crowned King Elessar. Through Aragorn, the ancient kingdoms of Arnor and Gondor were reunited.

Bree-land consequently became part of the Reunited Kingdom, restoring royal authority to a region that had lived without a king for more than a thousand years.

When Gandalf and the Hobbits passed through Bree on their journey home, they told Butterbur that better times were coming. Gandalf specifically reassured him that the King would again travel the northern lands and that the roads would become safer.

For Bree, this represented the restoration of the political world that had vanished with Arthedain. The old North-South Road and Great East Road no longer passed only through the ruins of a lost kingdom; they now lay within the realm of a king who intended to restore the North.

The Crossroads Endure

Bree's importance in the history of Middle-earth did not come from military power or great rulers. It survived precisely because it was an ordinary settlement in a region where many greater places did not.

It existed before the founding of the Shire and endured through the division of Arnor, the wars with Angmar, the fall of Arthedain, and the long centuries in which the Rangers guarded Eriador without a kingdom of their own. Men and Hobbits continued living together beneath Bree-hill while the great roads around them became quieter and the cities of the Dúnedain fell into ruin.

Its position at the crossroads also gave Bree an importance disproportionate to its size. Marcho and Blanco came from the Bree region before founding the Shire. Aragorn was known there long before he became king. Frodo met his future guide there because the Prancing Pony remained one of the few active stopping places on the road between the Shire and Rivendell.

By the beginning of the Fourth Age, Bree had outlasted kingdoms, wars, and centuries of decline. With the restoration of Arnor under King Elessar, it entered a new age as one of the oldest continuously inhabited communities in the restored northern realm.

For much of its history, Bree had stood at the crossroads of roads leading to places greater than itself.

Its achievement was that it remained there.
