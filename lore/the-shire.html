The Coming of the Hobbits

Long before the Shire had a name, Hobbits dwelt east of the Misty Mountains, in lands about the upper reaches of the Anduin. Of their earliest history little was remembered, for theirs was never a people greatly concerned with chronicles, kings, or the quarrels of the mighty.

In time they began to wander westward. The three ancient kindreds of Hobbit-kind—the Harfoots, Stoors, and Fallohides—crossed the mountains by different roads and settled for a while in Eriador. There they lived among, or near, the realms of Men, until at last a country was found that suited them exceedingly well.

In the year 1601 of the Third Age, the Fallohide brothers Marcho and Blanco received permission from Argeleb II, King of Arthedain, to cross the Baranduin and settle the lands beyond. With many followers they passed over the great stone bridge and entered the country lying between the river and the Far Downs.

In return, the Hobbits were required to acknowledge the King, maintain the bridge, and speed his messengers upon their journeys.

Thus began the Shire-reckoning, and thus the Shire itself.

A Country Made for Hobbits

The new land proved well suited to its inhabitants. It stretched some forty leagues from the Brandywine Bridge to the Far Downs, and about fifty leagues from the northern moors to the marshes in the south.

The Hobbits divided it into four great districts called the Farthings: Northfarthing, Southfarthing, Eastfarthing, and Westfarthing. Within them arose villages, farms, mills, gardens, inns, and innumerable Hobbit-holes.

The Southfarthing became particularly famous for pipe-weed, including such celebrated varieties as Longbottom Leaf and Old Toby. In the Westfarthing lay Hobbiton and Bywater, while farther south stood Tuckborough and the Great Smials of the Took family.

Beyond the eastern border lay Buckland, founded when Gorhendad Oldbuck crossed the Brandywine and settled the land between the river and the Old Forest. His descendants became the Brandybucks, a family regarded by many respectable Shire Hobbits as adventurous almost to the point of impropriety.

For the most part, however, the people of the Shire desired neither adventure nor renown. A full pantry, good tilled earth, a comfortable chair beside the fire, and sufficient time for conversation were generally held to be better things.

The King Who Vanished

For centuries the Shire remained nominally subject to the Kings of Arthedain. Yet the wars of the North seldom troubled Hobbit life directly.

Then, in 1974 of the Third Age, the northern kingdom fell beneath the assault of Angmar. King Arvedui, the last king of Arthedain, perished soon afterward, and no king returned to rule the North for more than a thousand years.

The Hobbits did not therefore declare themselves kings.

They simply carried on.

In the absence of the monarchy, the office of Thain arose to uphold the authority that had once belonged to the King. Eventually the Thainship passed to the Took family, whose head resided at the Great Smials.

Other offices remained deliberately modest. The Mayor of Michel Delving, elected every seven years at the Free Fair on the White Downs, was among the most important officials in the Shire. There were also a small number of Shirriffs, concerned chiefly with stray beasts and keeping order, and the Bounders, who watched the borders.

For a people living in a world of Dark Lords, dragons, warring kingdoms, and ancient curses, the Hobbits had developed a remarkably effective system of government based largely upon having very little government at all.

The Watchers Beyond the Bounds

The Shire-folk came to believe that the troubles of the outside world had little to do with them. In truth, their peace was not maintained by good fortune alone.

After the fall of the northern kingdom, the surviving Dúnedain of the North became a wandering people. Their Rangers secretly guarded the lands of Eriador, and the Shire lay within their watch.

Few Hobbits understood this. Rangers were generally regarded as mysterious and rather disreputable wanderers.

Yet while fires burned comfortably in Hobbiton and crops ripened beneath the summer sun, armed watchers walked the lonely country beyond the borders.

The Shire slept because others did not.

Wolves, Winter, and Goblins

Even the Shire could not escape every shadow.

In 2747, Orcs from Mount Gram invaded the Northfarthing under their leader Golfimbul. They were met at the Battle of Greenfields by Hobbits led by Bandobras Took, called the Bullroarer.

Bandobras was exceptionally large for a Hobbit and famous thereafter as one of the Shire's greatest heroes. The Orcs were defeated, and no battle would again be fought within the Shire for more than two centuries.

Greater suffering came with the Long Winter of 2758–59. Bitter cold and famine afflicted Eriador, and many Hobbits died. Gandalf the Grey came to their aid during those desperate months and discovered beneath their unassuming manners a resilience and courage that he would not forget.

Another terrible winter arrived in 2911, when the Brandywine froze and white wolves crossed into the Shire.

But such disasters passed into memory, and ordinary life returned—as ordinary life usually did in the Shire.

The Shadow Draws Near

Unknown to almost every Hobbit, events of enormous consequence had already begun within their borders.

In 2941, a respectable Hobbit of Bag End named Bilbo Baggins departed Hobbiton in the company of Gandalf and thirteen Dwarves. He returned the following year considerably richer, somewhat stranger, and in possession of a small golden ring.

For decades the ring appeared to be nothing more than a useful curiosity.

It was not.

Bilbo had brought into the Shire the One Ring of Sauron.

When Gandalf eventually discovered its true nature, Bilbo's heir Frodo Baggins was forced to flee his home. In 3018, servants of Sauron entered the Shire itself: the Nazgûl, searching for a Baggins and the Ring he carried.

Frodo escaped with Samwise Gamgee and Peregrin Took, soon joined by Meriadoc Brandybuck. Their road would carry them far beyond everything they had known, into the heart of the War of the Ring.

And for a time, the Shire was left behind.

The Scouring of the Shire

While the four travellers were away, the Shire changed.

The Rangers who had long guarded its borders had ridden south, and outsiders began to enter the country. Ruffians seized control under the authority of a mysterious figure called Sharkey.

When Frodo, Sam, Merry, and Pippin finally returned in 3019, they found gates where roads had once been open, trees cut down, buildings ruined, ugly new structures raised, and Hobbit life subjected to petty rules and intimidation.

Behind it all stood Saruman.

He had survived the overthrow of Sauron only to seek a final, spiteful revenge upon the Hobbits.

But the four who returned were no longer the inexperienced travellers who had departed.

Merry and Pippin rallied the Shire-folk, and at the Battle of Bywater the ruffians were defeated. Frodo, though unwilling to take vengeance upon Saruman, could not prevent the fallen wizard's death at the hands of his own servant, Gríma.

The War of the Ring was over.

Yet the Shire had wounds of its own to heal.

The Great Year

Samwise Gamgee had carried home a gift from Galadriel: a small box containing earth from her orchard and a silver mallorn seed.

He scattered the earth throughout the Shire wherever trees had been destroyed, reserving the seed for the Party Field in Hobbiton.

What followed became remembered as the Great Year of Plenty.

Trees flourished. Crops prospered. Children were born in remarkable numbers. And in the Party Field there grew a golden-blossomed mallorn, the only one of its kind west of the Mountains and east of the Sea.

The Shire became green again.

Under the King

The Hobbits had long spoken half-jokingly of "the King," though for a thousand years no king had ruled in the North.

Now one had returned.

Aragorn II Elessar, descendant of the ancient kings and friend to the four Hobbits, became King of the Reunited Kingdom. In the fifteenth year of the Fourth Age, he declared the Shire a Free Land under the protection of the Northern Sceptre and prohibited Men from entering it.

The King himself honored that law.

Later, in Fourth Age 31, he granted the Hobbits the lands westward from the Far Downs to the Tower Hills. This territory became known as the Westmarch, and the Shire reached its greatest extent.

Samwise Gamgee served many terms as Mayor. Peregrin Took became Thain. Meriadoc Brandybuck became Master of Buckland. Their families prospered, and the deeds of the Ring-bearers and their companions passed from living memory into history.

Yet perhaps the greatest victory of the Shire was that, after everything that had happened, it remained recognizably itself.

Gardens were planted. Ale was brewed. Birthdays were celebrated. Meals were taken whenever a reasonable excuse could be found for them.

Beyond its borders, kingdoms rose again and the long dominion of Men began.

But behind its hedges and green hills endured a small country whose people had never sought a place in the histories of the great—and who, when history finally came knocking at their round doors, proved themselves greater than anyone had expected.
