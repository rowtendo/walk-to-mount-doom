The Coming of the Hobbits

The people later known as Hobbits did not originally live in the Shire. In the early Third Age, their ancestors dwelt farther east, especially in and around the upper valleys of the Anduin. Tolkien divides these early Hobbits into three broad groups: the Harfoots, Stoors, and Fallohides, each with somewhat different customs and preferences.

Over time, Hobbit populations moved westward across the Misty Mountains and entered Eriador. The Harfoots appear to have crossed first, followed later by Stoors and Fallohides, and by the middle centuries of the Third Age Hobbits were living in places such as Bree and the surrounding country.

This migration brought them into lands governed by the northern Dúnedain. Although the Hobbits generally avoided the great political affairs of Men, their eventual settlement west of the Brandywine took place under the authority of the kings of Arthedain.

The Founding of the Shire

In Third Age 1601, the brothers Marcho and Blanco, leaders among the Fallohide Hobbits, received permission from King Argeleb II of Arthedain to cross the river Baranduin and settle the lands beyond it. The Hobbits called the river the Brandywine, and their new country became known as the Shire.

The grant was not entirely without obligations. The Hobbits were expected to recognize the authority of the king, maintain the great bridge over the Brandywine, and assist the king's messengers when necessary. These duties were light, and the Hobbits were largely left to govern their own affairs.

The founding year became so important that the Hobbits later counted their own calendar from it. Third Age 1601 became Shire-reckoning 1, and events within the Shire were thereafter commonly dated according to this local system.

The Shape of the Shire

The original Shire stretched roughly forty leagues from east to west and fifty leagues from north to south. Its eastern boundary was the Brandywine, while the western edge lay near the Far Downs. The land contained fertile fields, wooded hills, streams, villages, and farm country well suited to Hobbit life.

The Shire was divided into four Farthings: Northfarthing, Southfarthing, Eastfarthing, and Westfarthing. These divisions were administrative rather than political in the sense of independent territories, and together they formed a single Hobbit country.

Different parts of the Shire became known for particular settlements and traditions. The Westfarthing included Hobbiton, Bywater, and Tuckborough, while the Southfarthing became famous for pipe-weed grown around Longbottom. The Took family was centered at the Great Smials in Tuckborough, while the Baggins family became closely associated with Hobbiton.

Buckland

East of the Brandywine lay Buckland, a Hobbit settlement that developed separately from the original four Farthings. It was founded by Gorhendad Oldbuck, who crossed the river and established Brandy Hall at Bucklebury.

The Oldbucks later became known as the Brandybucks, and Buckland developed its own customs. Its inhabitants were generally regarded by other Hobbits as somewhat unusual because of their proximity to the Old Forest and their greater willingness to use boats.

Although Buckland was closely associated with the Shire, it lay outside the original boundaries granted by Argeleb II. Its history therefore illustrates how Hobbit settlement gradually expanded beyond the first territorial limits of the Shire.

Government Without a King

For the first centuries of the Shire's history, the Hobbits formally remained subjects of the kings of Arthedain. In practice, however, royal interference in their daily affairs appears to have been very limited.

When the North-kingdom weakened and eventually disappeared, the Hobbits adapted by preserving local offices and customs. The most important hereditary office became that of the Thain, originally established to represent the authority of the absent king and to lead the Shire in times of emergency.

The office eventually passed to the Took family, whose head became Thain of the Shire. Even then, the Thain had relatively little day-to-day authority compared with the rulers of other realms in Middle-earth, reflecting the Hobbits' strong preference for local custom and informal government.

The Mayor, Shirriffs, and Bounders

The other principal office was the Mayor of Michel Delving, elected every seven years at the Free Fair on the White Downs. The Mayor presided over public events and also held the offices of Postmaster and First Shirriff.

The Shire maintained only a very small law-enforcement system. A limited number of Shirriffs dealt with local disputes and minor disorder, while Bounders watched the borders and kept an eye on unfamiliar outsiders.

This system worked largely because serious crime was rare. Hobbit society depended more on custom, family reputation, and community expectations than on large institutions of government.

The Fall of Arthedain

The wider political world around the Shire changed dramatically in the later Third Age. The kingdom of Arthedain, which had originally authorized the settlement of the Shire, was gradually weakened by centuries of conflict with Angmar.

In Third Age 1974, the Witch-king captured Fornost and brought the North-kingdom to an end. Although Angmar was defeated the following year, the kingdom of Arthedain was not immediately restored.

The Hobbits therefore continued living without an active king. Their local institutions remained in place, and for most inhabitants of the Shire the disappearance of the North-kingdom became a distant historical memory rather than an immediate political crisis.

The Rangers Beyond the Borders

After the fall of Arthedain, the surviving Dúnedain became the Rangers of the North. Their chieftains were descendants of Isildur, but they no longer ruled openly from cities or courts.

The Rangers continued to patrol Eriador and protected settlements such as Bree and the Shire from threats that most Hobbits never fully understood. Orcs, wolves, trolls, and other dangers were often kept far from the borders before they could trouble the Shire directly.

This hidden protection became one of the reasons the Shire remained so peaceful despite the growing emptiness and danger of the surrounding lands. Most Hobbits knew little about the Rangers and did not realize how much of their security depended upon them.

The Battle of Greenfields

The Shire was not entirely free from violence. In Third Age 2747, a band of Orcs led by Golfimbul entered the Northfarthing and was confronted near Greenfields.

The Hobbits were led by Bandobras Took, known as Bullroarer, a famously large and formidable member of the Took family. The Orcs were defeated, and Golfimbul was killed during the battle.

The Battle of Greenfields became one of the rare military episodes remembered in Shire history. It also contributed to the Took family's reputation for producing Hobbits with an unusual willingness to become involved in dangerous affairs.

The Long Winter

In Third Age 2758–2759, Eriador experienced the severe Long Winter. Heavy snow, cold, and food shortages affected much of the North, including the Shire.

The winter caused considerable hardship, and many Hobbits suffered from hunger. Gandalf became involved in helping the Shire during this period, a circumstance that contributed to his long familiarity with Hobbit society.

The event remained memorable because the Shire was otherwise relatively sheltered from the large disasters affecting the wider world. Even so, weather and famine could still reach a country protected from war.

The Fell Winter

Another severe winter struck in Third Age 2911, later remembered as the Fell Winter. The Brandywine froze, and white wolves crossed the river into the Shire.

The event was dangerous enough to be remembered generations later. It also showed that the Brandywine, usually one of the Shire's principal natural boundaries, could not always protect the country from threats moving out of the wilderness.

Unlike the wars that had destroyed Arnor, however, the Fell Winter did not permanently alter Hobbit society. The Shire recovered and returned to its usual pattern of farming, family life, and local affairs.

Pipe-weed and Shire Agriculture

One of the most distinctive agricultural developments in the Shire was the cultivation of pipe-weed. The plant was first grown successfully by Tobold Hornblower of Longbottom in the Southfarthing during the Third Age.

Varieties such as Longbottom Leaf, Old Toby, and Southern Star became famous among Hobbits. The custom of smoking pipe-weed spread beyond the Shire and was eventually adopted by travelers and figures such as Gandalf and Aragorn.

Agriculture more generally remained the foundation of Shire life. Farming, gardening, milling, brewing, and other local trades supported a society that had little interest in large-scale commerce or industry.

Bilbo Baggins and the Wider World

For most Hobbits, the events of the wider world remained remote until the adventures of Bilbo Baggins. In Third Age 2941, Bilbo left Hobbiton with Gandalf and Thorin Oakenshield's company of Dwarves on the quest to reclaim Erebor.

His journey took him far beyond the borders of the Shire and brought him into contact with trolls, Elves, Goblins, Gollum, and Smaug. During that journey, Bilbo acquired the One Ring, although he did not understand its true nature.

When he returned, many Hobbits considered him eccentric because of his disappearance and his association with outsiders. Nevertheless, his adventure created the first direct connection between Bag End and events that would eventually determine the fate of Middle-earth.

Frodo and the Ring

Bilbo eventually left the Shire in Third Age 3001, leaving Bag End and most of his possessions to Frodo Baggins. The Ring remained behind with Frodo.

Years later, Gandalf confirmed that Bilbo's ring was in fact the One Ring of Sauron. This discovery transformed the Shire from an isolated country at the edge of events into the starting point of the most important journey of the age.

In 3018, Frodo left the Shire with Sam Gamgee and was later joined by Merry Brandybuck and Pippin Took. Their departure brought four Hobbits into the War of the Ring and ultimately connected the fate of the Shire with the defeat of Sauron.

The Black Riders Enter the Shire

Before Frodo escaped, the Nazgûl entered the Shire searching for a Hobbit named Baggins. Their presence revealed how far Sauron's influence had reached and how vulnerable the country could become once its location and connection to the Ring were known.

The Black Riders questioned Hobbits and followed Frodo's trail through the Eastfarthing and toward Buckland. Frodo and his companions avoided capture and left the Shire through the Old Forest rather than using the main roads.

The episode marked one of the first direct intrusions of Sauron's servants into the Shire. Yet it was not the last or most destructive outside influence to reach the country during the War of the Ring.

The Absence of the Rangers

As the war developed, the Rangers of the North were drawn away from their usual patrols. Many rode south with Halbarad and the Grey Company to join Aragorn.

Their departure removed much of the quiet protection that had helped keep the Shire secure for generations. At the same time, broader lawlessness spread through Eriador as Sauron's war disrupted the existing balance of power.

These conditions made the Shire increasingly vulnerable to outsiders. While Frodo, Sam, Merry, and Pippin were far away, Men began entering the country in growing numbers.

Saruman and the Shire

After his defeat at Isengard, Saruman eventually turned his attention toward the Shire. He already had connections with Men operating there and had long taken an interest in the region, partly because of Gandalf's affection for Hobbits and partly because Saruman himself had secretly acquired pipe-weed.

By the time the four travelers returned home in 3019, Saruman had taken control under the name Sharkey. Ruffians enforced new rules, confiscated food and goods, cut down trees, damaged buildings, and imposed an unfamiliar system of control on the inhabitants.

The occupation represented a direct assault on the social character of the Shire. It replaced local custom with coercion and introduced both violence and destructive industry into a society that had previously known very little of either.

The Scouring of the Shire

Frodo, Sam, Merry, and Pippin returned to the Shire in November 3019 and immediately found the country transformed. Their experiences during the War of the Ring had changed them, and they were far better prepared to confront the ruffians than they would have been before leaving home.

Merry and Pippin helped organize resistance among the Hobbits, while Sam and Frodo also played important roles. The conflict culminated in the Battle of Bywater, where the ruffians were defeated.

Frodo tried to prevent unnecessary killing and repeatedly argued for mercy, even toward Saruman. After the battle, Saruman was confronted at Bag End, where Gríma Wormtongue eventually killed him before being shot by Hobbit archers.

The Restoration of the Shire

The defeat of Saruman's Men did not immediately undo the damage. Trees had been cut down, buildings altered or destroyed, and parts of the countryside had been scarred by the occupation.

Sam Gamgee played the leading role in restoring the land. He used the small box of earth given to him by Galadriel in Lothlórien, spreading it carefully across the Shire where damage had been greatest.

He also planted the mallorn seed Galadriel had given him in the Party Field where the old Party Tree had stood. The mallorn grew successfully and became one of the most remarkable trees in the Shire.

The Great Year of Plenty

The year 1420 by Shire-reckoning, corresponding largely to Third Age 3020, became remembered as the Great Year of Plenty. Crops flourished, fruit was abundant, and the recovery of the Shire proceeded rapidly.

Sam's use of Galadriel's gift became associated with this extraordinary renewal. The Shire emerged from the Scouring not merely repaired but, in some respects, more beautiful and fruitful than before.

The Great Year also symbolized the difference between restoration and simple return. The Hobbits could not completely erase what had happened, but they were able to rebuild their country without surrendering its basic character.

Frodo's Departure

Although the Shire recovered, Frodo did not recover in the same way. The Morgul wound from Weathertop, the sting of Shelob, and the psychological burden of carrying the Ring continued to affect him.

In Third Age 3021, Frodo left the Shire with Bilbo and traveled to the Grey Havens. He sailed into the West with Gandalf, Elrond, and Galadriel.

Frodo's departure created a striking contrast with the restoration around him. The Shire had been healed enough for others to remain and build new lives, but Frodo's wounds were too deep for him to find complete peace there.

The Shire Under King Elessar

After the defeat of Sauron, Aragorn became King Elessar and restored the northern kingdom as part of the Reunited Kingdom. The Shire therefore once again existed within a realm ruled by the heir of the kings who had originally granted the Hobbits their land.

In the early Fourth Age, Elessar formally confirmed the Shire's special status. In Fourth Age 15, he declared it a Free Land under the protection of the Northern Sceptre and forbade Men from entering it without permission.

This arrangement preserved Hobbit self-government while placing the Shire under royal protection. It also reflected Aragorn's long knowledge of the country and his recognition that its distinct way of life should be allowed to continue.

The Westmarch

The Shire later expanded westward. In Fourth Age 31, King Elessar granted the Hobbits the lands between the Far Downs and the Tower Hills.

This region became known as the Westmarch and was incorporated into the Shire. The expansion extended Hobbit settlement farther toward the Grey Havens and represented one of the most important territorial changes since the original founding.

The Westmarch eventually became associated with members of the Fairbairn family, descendants of Sam Gamgee. In this way, the expansion of the Shire became connected with the same generation that had helped restore it after the War of the Ring.

Sam, Merry, and Pippin

The three surviving companions of Frodo became important figures in Shire society during the Fourth Age. Samwise Gamgee was elected Mayor of Michel Delving repeatedly and served a total of seven terms.

Peregrin Took later became Thain, while Meriadoc Brandybuck became Master of Buckland. Their positions placed three members of the Fellowship at the center of the Shire's principal institutions.

Their influence also helped connect the Shire more closely with the Reunited Kingdom. Merry and Pippin maintained ties with Rohan and Gondor, while Sam's family became prominent within the Shire itself.

The Shire in the Fourth Age

The beginning of the Fourth Age brought the Shire a level of security it had not enjoyed for centuries. The Rangers were no longer merely a hidden remnant guarding Eriador, because their chieftain had become king and the northern realm had been restored.

At the same time, the Shire retained its own institutions, traditions, and local government. It did not become a province administered directly by Men, and the royal prohibition on outsiders entering the country helped preserve its independence.

This balance allowed the Shire to remain culturally distinct while benefiting from the renewed stability of the Reunited Kingdom. The wider world had changed dramatically, but Hobbit society continued on largely according to its own customs.

The Halfling Realm

The history of the Shire was defined less by conquest than by continuity. It began as a royal grant within the kingdom of Arthedain and survived the destruction of that kingdom, centuries of depopulation across Eriador, severe winters, Orc attacks, and the eventual return of Sauron.

Its isolation was never complete. The Rangers guarded its borders, Gandalf maintained a long interest in its people, Bilbo carried the One Ring home from his travels, and Frodo, Sam, Merry, and Pippin eventually carried the concerns of the wider world out of the Shire and then brought their experience back again.

The Scouring of the Shire proved that even the most sheltered place in Middle-earth could not remain entirely untouched by war. Yet the Hobbits' response also demonstrated that the Shire's strength did not depend upon armies or fortifications. Its survival rested on community, memory, local loyalty, and the ability to restore what had been damaged without abandoning the society that had existed before.

By the Fourth Age, the Shire had entered a new political setting under King Elessar while preserving the character that had defined it for more than fourteen centuries. Its history had begun under a king, continued for generations without one, and ended the Third Age under the protection of a restored northern crown.
