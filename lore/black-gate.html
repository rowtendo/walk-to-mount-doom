The Northwestern Entrance to Mordor

The Black Gate, called the Morannon in Sindarin, guarded the principal northwestern entrance into Mordor. It stood across Cirith Gorgor, the Haunted Pass, where the mountain ranges of the Ephel Dúath and Ered Lithui approached one another and created a natural gateway into the interior of Sauron's realm.

Beyond the pass lay the plateau of Gorgoroth, with Barad-dûr and Mount Doom farther within Mordor. Outside the gate, roads approached from the north and west, making Cirith Gorgor strategically important to anyone attempting either to enter Mordor or to defend it against invasion.

By the end of the Third Age, the Morannon had become one of the most formidable fortifications associated with Sauron. Its history, however, included an earlier period in which the defenses around the pass belonged not to Mordor but to Gondor, whose soldiers attempted to prevent Sauron's return after his first overthrow.

Sauron's First Kingdom in Mordor

Sauron established himself in Mordor around Second Age 1000, choosing the enclosed country as the center of his growing power. Its surrounding mountains provided strong natural defenses, while Orodruin, later called Mount Doom, supplied the fires he used for some of his greatest works.

During the following centuries, Sauron built Barad-dûr and developed Mordor into the principal stronghold of his power. After forging the One Ring around Second Age 1600, he became openly involved in wars against the Elves and later against the Númenóreans and their descendants.

The northwestern entrance through Cirith Gorgor consequently became one of the most important approaches to Sauron's territory. Tolkien does not provide a detailed architectural history of the gate during the Second Age, so the precise form of the defenses there before Sauron's first defeat is uncertain.

The Last Alliance

At the end of the Second Age, Gil-galad and Elendil formed the Last Alliance of Elves and Men against Sauron. Their armies crossed the Misty Mountains, fought Sauron's forces at the Battle of Dagorlad, and then entered Mordor.

The campaign culminated in a siege of Barad-dûr lasting seven years. In Second Age 3441, Sauron was overthrown, though Gil-galad and Elendil were both killed in the final struggle. Isildur cut the One Ring from Sauron's hand and claimed it rather than destroying it.

Sauron's defeat left the victorious Dúnedain with the problem of preventing Mordor from becoming a threat again. Gondor therefore established fortifications and garrisons around the entrances into the land, including the strategically important pass of Cirith Gorgor.

Gondor's Watch on Mordor

During the early Third Age, Gondor maintained a military watch upon Mordor. The purpose of these defenses was to prevent Sauron's servants from returning and to ensure that the defeated Dark Lord could not quietly rebuild his former realm.

At Cirith Gorgor, Gondor constructed the two great towers later known as the Towers of the Teeth, Carchost and Narchost. They stood on either side of the entrance and formed part of the defensive system controlling the pass.

This origin is important because the towers later appeared to be natural parts of Sauron's frontier. In fact, they had originally been built by his enemies and were intended to keep watch upon the land he had lost.

For centuries after the Last Alliance, Gondorian soldiers continued to guard Mordor. As long as that watch remained strong, the northwestern entrance was controlled from outside rather than from within.

The Decline of the Watch

Gondor's ability to maintain distant fortifications weakened during the Third Age. The kingdom suffered civil conflict during the Kin-strife, repeated wars with Easterlings and other enemies, and eventually the catastrophic Great Plague of Third Age 1636.

The plague killed a large part of Gondor's population and forced the kingdom to abandon a number of remote positions. The watch on Mordor weakened substantially during this period, and the fortifications surrounding the land were no longer maintained with their former strength.

As Gondor's power declined further, the old guard over Mordor eventually failed. The towers and passes that had once been intended to prevent evil from returning were left without sufficient defenders.

This abandonment created the conditions under which Sauron's servants could gradually return to Mordor. The Morannon and the Towers of the Teeth would eventually be occupied by the very power they had originally been built to contain.

The Nazgûl Return to Mordor

In Third Age 1980, the Witch-king of Angmar entered Mordor and gathered the other Nazgûl there. Their arrival marked an important stage in the reoccupation of Sauron's former realm.

The Ringwraiths operated from Mordor while Sauron himself remained concealed elsewhere. In 2000, they attacked Minas Ithil, and after a two-year siege they captured the city in 2002. Minas Ithil thereafter became Minas Morgul, one of the principal strongholds of Sauron's servants.

During the centuries that followed, Mordor increasingly returned to hostile control. Gondor could no longer maintain the old watch, and the northern entrance became part of the growing military system that would eventually defend Sauron's restored kingdom.

Sauron Returns Openly

Sauron had spent centuries rebuilding his strength at Dol Guldur in southern Mirkwood, where he was known for a time as the Necromancer. In Third Age 2941, the White Council moved against Dol Guldur, but Sauron had anticipated the attack and withdrew.

He returned secretly to Mordor and in 2951 declared himself openly. Barad-dûr was rebuilt, Mount Doom became active again, and Sauron began preparing his realm for the war he expected against Gondor and the other Free Peoples.

The old Gondorian defenses at Cirith Gorgor were incorporated into this renewed system. Carchost and Narchost, originally built to watch Mordor, became towers guarding its entrance, while the Morannon itself became a heavily defended barrier across the pass.

By the time of the War of the Ring, entering Mordor through the Black Gate required passing directly through one of the most closely guarded positions in Sauron's entire realm.

The Black Gate in the War of the Ring

The Morannon consisted of a great fortified barrier controlling the gap between the mountains. Massive gates closed the principal road into Mordor, while walls, battlements, towers, and surrounding fortifications allowed Sauron's forces to dominate the approaches.

The Towers of the Teeth stood on the heights to either side. Roads from the towers descended toward the gate, allowing troops to reinforce the pass and observe movement across the surrounding country.

North of the Morannon stretched the desolate lands around the Dagorlad, while the Dead Marshes lay farther west. Armies approaching Mordor from the north therefore had to cross exposed and hostile country before reaching an entrance that was itself heavily fortified.

For Frodo and Sam, this geography made the Morannon the most obvious route into Mordor but also the least practical. Their journey toward the gate demonstrated how effectively Sauron had transformed the old northern approach into a controlled military frontier.

Frodo and Sam Approach the Morannon

In March 3019, Frodo Baggins and Samwise Gamgee traveled toward Mordor under the guidance of Gollum. After crossing the Dead Marshes and the desolate lands beyond them, they reached a position from which they could observe the Black Gate.

Their original intention was to enter Mordor through the Morannon because neither Frodo nor Sam knew another route. Once they saw the fortifications and the forces moving through them, however, it became clear that two Hobbits could not simply pass through the gate unnoticed.

The gate was active with military traffic. Sauron was assembling forces for the coming war, and troops from different regions were entering Mordor or moving through its northern defenses.

Attempting to cross openly would almost certainly have led to capture. Frodo nevertheless felt obligated to continue because he had been charged with taking the Ring into Mordor, even when the apparent route seemed impossible.

Gollum's Alternative

Gollum opposed Frodo's attempt to approach the Black Gate directly. He knew of another route into Mordor farther south and urged the Hobbits to follow him instead.

That alternative led toward Minas Morgul, the pass of Cirith Ungol, and the tunnels inhabited by Shelob. Gollum concealed the full danger of this route because he intended to use Shelob to kill Frodo and Sam and recover the Ring afterward.

Frodo eventually accepted the alternative because the Morannon offered no realistic possibility of secret entry. The three travelers therefore turned south through Ithilien rather than attempting to cross the gate.

This decision determined the remainder of Frodo and Sam's route to Mount Doom. The strength of the Black Gate forced them toward Cirith Ungol, where they encountered some of the greatest dangers of their journey.

Sauron's Northern Armies

While Frodo and Sam moved south, the Morannon remained an important center for Sauron's military preparations. Mordor was not an isolated fortress but the center of a network of subject peoples and allied armies extending into the east and south.

Forces from these regions moved along roads connected with the Black Gate and the lands outside it. Sauron's war against Gondor depended not only upon Orcs bred within Mordor but also upon Easterlings, Haradrim, and other peoples who fought under his command or influence.

The Morannon therefore served both as a defensive entrance and as a point through which armies could move between Mordor and the northern theaters of war. Its importance increased as Sauron committed more of his strength to the campaign against Gondor.

The Captains of the West

After the Battle of the Pelennor Fields, the leaders of the Free Peoples understood that military victory over Sauron by conventional means was impossible. Even after the destruction of his armies outside Minas Tirith, Sauron retained far greater forces than Gondor and Rohan could assemble.

Gandalf and Aragorn therefore proposed a different strategy. They would march openly against the Black Gate, drawing Sauron's attention toward themselves and away from the interior of Mordor.

The plan depended entirely upon Frodo. The Captains of the West did not know whether he was alive or how close he might be to Mount Doom, but they understood that the destruction of the Ring offered their only real hope of defeating Sauron.

Their march toward the Morannon was therefore designed as a diversion rather than an attempt to conquer Mordor. By appearing to challenge Sauron directly, they hoped to convince him that Aragorn possessed the Ring and intended to use it.

The March to the Black Gate

The Host of the West left Minas Tirith on 18 March 3019. It initially numbered about seven thousand fighting men, a deliberately small force compared with the armies Sauron could still command.

The army traveled through Ithilien and approached the northern entrance of Mordor. Along the way, the Captains deliberately announced the return of the King and challenged Sauron's authority, strengthening the impression that their march was an act of confidence rather than desperation.

Some soldiers were overcome by fear before reaching the Morannon. Aragorn released those who could not continue and assigned them other tasks rather than forcing them onward.

By the time the army reached the Black Gate, it was even smaller than when it had left Minas Tirith. Its purpose, however, did not depend upon numbers; it needed only to hold Sauron's attention for as long as possible.

The Mouth of Sauron

On 25 March 3019, the Host of the West stood before the Morannon. The Black Gate opened, but instead of immediately releasing an army, Sauron sent a messenger to negotiate.

The emissary called himself the Mouth of Sauron. He was a Black Númenórean who had entered Sauron's service and had risen to a position of considerable authority, though Tolkien does not give his personal name.

He displayed possessions taken from Frodo and Sam, including Frodo's mithril coat, Sam's sword, and a grey Elven-cloak. These items had been captured after Frodo was taken at Cirith Ungol, and Sauron's servants used them in an attempt to convince the Captains that the Ring-bearer's mission had failed.

The Mouth of Sauron did not actually know Frodo's purpose or the location of the Ring. Sauron had not yet understood that his enemies intended to destroy it, which allowed Gandalf to recognize that the messenger's claims did not prove the quest had failed.

Sauron's Terms

The Mouth of Sauron demanded that the Captains surrender and accept terms that would effectively destroy the independence of the western kingdoms. Gondor and its allies would be required to withdraw, surrender territory, and submit to political conditions imposed by Sauron.

Gandalf rejected the demands. He also took back the possessions of Frodo and Sam, preventing Sauron's messenger from using them further as instruments of intimidation.

The exchange confirmed that Sauron still believed his enemies were acting through conventional political and military ambitions. He interpreted Aragorn's march as the challenge of a rival seeking power rather than as a deliberate sacrifice intended to assist the Ring-bearer.

With negotiations ended, the Morannon opened and Sauron's armies moved out to surround the Host of the West.

The Battle of the Morannon

Sauron's forces greatly outnumbered the army of the West. Orcs and other troops emerged from the Black Gate, while additional forces descended from the surrounding hills and the Towers of the Teeth.

The Captains formed their army on two defensive hills outside the gate. Aragorn, Gandalf, Éomer, Imrahil, and the other leaders prepared to hold their positions even though they had little expectation of surviving a prolonged battle.

The fighting became known as the Battle of the Morannon, the final major battle of the War of the Ring. Its strategic purpose was not to defeat Sauron's armies but to keep his attention fixed upon the challenge outside his gate.

The plan succeeded. Sauron concentrated his forces and his attention on the Captains of the West while Frodo and Sam moved across Gorgoroth toward Mount Doom.

The Destruction of the One Ring

While the battle continued outside the Black Gate, Frodo and Sam reached the Sammath Naur inside Mount Doom. Frodo ultimately claimed the Ring rather than destroying it, but Gollum attacked him, seized it, and fell into the fire.

The destruction of the One Ring ended the power Sauron had invested in it. Barad-dûr collapsed, Sauron's ability to dominate his servants was broken, and the Nazgûl were destroyed in the eruption of Mount Doom.

The consequences reached the battlefield immediately. Sauron's armies lost the controlling will that had driven them, and many fled, surrendered, or were destroyed.

The Host of the West had marched to the Morannon expecting that its survival depended upon events taking place far inside Mordor. When the Ring was destroyed, the diversion achieved its purpose and Sauron's military power collapsed with him.

The Fall of the Black Gate

The destruction of Sauron's power also brought ruin to the fortifications guarding Mordor. The Towers of the Teeth and the Black Gate were overthrown amid the convulsions that followed the destruction of the Ring.

Their fall ended a long reversal in their history. The towers had originally been constructed by Gondor to watch Mordor after Sauron's defeat, then abandoned as Gondor weakened, and finally occupied by Sauron's servants as defenses for the realm they had once been intended to contain.

With Sauron permanently defeated, the Morannon no longer served as the fortified entrance to an enemy kingdom. Mordor's military system collapsed, and the lands that had been closed behind its defenses ceased to function as the center of a great imperial power.

The ruin of the gate therefore marked both the end of Sauron's restored Mordor and the end of the strategic purpose that Cirith Gorgor had held for much of the Second and Third Ages.

The Iron Threshold of Mordor

The history of the Black Gate was closely connected with the repeated rise and fall of Sauron's power. Cirith Gorgor had always formed one of the natural entrances into Mordor, but its political meaning changed depending upon who controlled the pass.

After the Last Alliance, Gondor fortified the region to prevent Sauron from returning. As Gondor weakened, that watch failed, and the Nazgûl gradually reoccupied Mordor. When Sauron returned openly in the final centuries of the Third Age, the old defenses were turned outward and incorporated into the military frontier of his restored realm.

During the War of the Ring, the Morannon shaped both sides of the final campaign. Its strength forced Frodo and Sam to seek another entrance through Cirith Ungol, while its strategic importance later made it the ideal place for Aragorn and Gandalf to challenge Sauron and draw his attention away from Mount Doom.

The Black Gate was therefore more than a barrier built to keep enemies outside Mordor. Its history reflected the larger struggle over the land itself: first Sauron's stronghold, then a territory watched by Gondor, then a realm slowly reclaimed by Sauron's servants, and finally the center of his last military defense. When the One Ring was destroyed on 25 March 3019, the Morannon fell with the power it had come to guard.
