The Founding of Khazad-dûm

Moria, originally called Khazad-dûm, was the greatest and most famous of the mansions of the Dwarves in Middle-earth. It lay beneath the central Misty Mountains and extended through a vast network of halls, mines, passages, stairways, bridges, and chambers beneath the peaks known as Caradhras, Celebdil, and Fanuidhol. Its eastern entrance opened toward the Dimrill Dale, while its western gate faced the lands of Eregion.

The kingdom was founded by Durin I, later called Durin the Deathless, the eldest of the Seven Fathers of the Dwarves. According to Dwarven tradition, Durin awoke at Mount Gundabad and later traveled south through the Misty Mountains until he came to a lake beneath three great peaks. Looking into its still waters, he saw stars reflected around his head like a crown.

The lake became known as Kheled-zâram, the Mirrormere, and the valley nearby as Azanulbizar, later called the Dimrill Dale. Durin established his dwelling in the mountains above the lake, and from that first settlement grew the realm of Khazad-dûm. The memory of Durin and the crown of stars remained central to the identity of his people throughout their history.

The Growth of the Dwarrowdelf

Khazad-dûm expanded during the First Age, though Tolkien gives relatively few details about its earliest political history. By the end of the Age, it had already become an important Dwarven realm, and its position beneath the Misty Mountains gave it access to lands on both sides of the range.

The destruction of Beleriand during the War of Wrath brought a major increase in its population. The old Dwarven cities of Nogrod and Belegost in the Blue Mountains were ruined or greatly diminished, and many of their inhabitants migrated eastward into Khazad-dûm. Their arrival increased both the wealth and the craftsmanship of Durin's realm.

By the Second Age, Khazad-dûm had become the greatest concentration of Dwarven power in Middle-earth. Its halls extended for many miles beneath the mountains, and its builders created chambers, roads, bridges, shafts, and stairways on a scale unmatched by any other Dwarven settlement.

Mithril

The greatest source of Khazad-dûm's wealth was mithril, a rare metal found there in extraordinary abundance. Mithril was light, exceptionally strong, and capable of being worked into objects of great beauty, making it highly prized by Dwarves, Elves, and Men.

By the later history of Middle-earth, Khazad-dûm was the only known source of mithril. The Dwarves called it true-silver, and the demand for it made the kingdom immensely wealthy. Its presence also encouraged the Dwarves to mine ever deeper beneath the mountains.

Mithril became associated with some of the most famous objects in Tolkien's world. A small coat of mithril was eventually given by Thorin Oakenshield to Bilbo Baggins, who later passed it to Frodo. That coat saved Frodo's life inside Moria when he was struck by an Orc spear.

The same mineral that made Khazad-dûm prosperous also contributed to the deep mining that eventually exposed a danger hidden far beneath the kingdom.

Eregion and the Elven-smiths

During the Second Age, the Elven realm of Eregion arose immediately west of Khazad-dûm. Its population included many Noldor, and its greatest craftsmen belonged to the Gwaith-i-Mírdain, led by Celebrimbor, grandson of Fëanor.

Relations between Eregion and Khazad-dûm were unusually friendly. The Elves valued Dwarven craftsmanship and access to mithril, while the Dwarves respected the skill of the Noldorin smiths. The two peoples traded, exchanged knowledge, and cooperated in ways rarely seen elsewhere in Middle-earth.

The best-known symbols of this friendship were Celebrimbor and the Dwarf-smith Narvi. Together they created the western entrance of Khazad-dûm, known later as the Doors of Durin, which reflected the openness that once existed between the Dwarven kingdom and the Elves of Eregion.

The Doors of Durin

The Doors of Durin were made during the Second Age at the western gate of Khazad-dûm. Narvi constructed the doors, while Celebrimbor drew the designs and inscription in ithildin, a material that shone in reflected moonlight and starlight.

The inscription identified the makers and gave the means of entry. The doors could be opened by speaking the Elvish word mellon, meaning "friend." In the prosperous days of Eregion and Khazad-dûm, the simplicity of that password reflected a period when the western gate stood between allied peoples rather than enemies.

The doors survived long after both political worlds had changed. By the time the Fellowship reached them in the Third Age, Eregion had been empty for thousands of years and Khazad-dûm had become Moria, yet the work of Narvi and Celebrimbor still remained.

The War of the Elves and Sauron

The friendship between Khazad-dûm and Eregion was disrupted by Sauron. After deceiving the Elven-smiths and secretly forging the One Ring, Sauron invaded Eriador in Second Age 1695 and attacked Eregion.

In 1697, Eregion was destroyed and Celebrimbor was killed. The Dwarves of Khazad-dûm fought against Sauron's forces and aided the Elves, but they could not prevent the destruction of the neighboring realm. Afterward, the Dwarves withdrew into their mountains and closed their western doors.

Sauron never forgot their resistance. Tolkien records that he hated the people of Khazad-dûm because of the aid they had given his enemies. Even so, the Dwarven kingdom survived the war and continued to prosper for many centuries after Eregion had vanished.

The Long Prosperity of Khazad-dûm

For much of the Second Age and the early Third Age, Khazad-dûm remained one of the strongest realms in Middle-earth. The kingdom's mines continued to produce wealth, its population remained large, and its halls extended ever deeper into the mountains.

This long period of prosperity created the impression that Khazad-dûm was nearly unassailable. External enemies had failed to destroy it, and its underground position protected it from many of the wars that devastated the surface lands.

The danger that eventually ended the kingdom did not come from an invading army. It came from something buried beneath the mountains since the end of the First Age.

The Balrog Beneath the Mountains

At the end of the First Age, Morgoth was defeated in the War of Wrath, and many of his servants were destroyed. At least one Balrog, however, escaped and fled into the depths beneath the Misty Mountains, where it remained hidden for thousands of years.

The Dwarves of Khazad-dûm had no knowledge of the creature's presence. As their mining continued, especially in pursuit of mithril, they delved ever deeper beneath Caradhras.

In Third Age 1980, their excavations awakened the Balrog. The event ended the long prosperity of Khazad-dûm with extraordinary speed.

Durin's Bane

The king of Khazad-dûm in Third Age 1980 was Durin VI. The Balrog emerged from the depths and killed him, and the Dwarves, not understanding exactly what the creature was, named it Durin's Bane.

The following year, Náin I, Durin's son and successor, was also killed. The Dwarves were unable to defeat the creature and abandoned Khazad-dûm in Third Age 1981, ending thousands of years of continuous settlement by Durin's Folk.

The survivors migrated north and east. Thráin I, son of Náin I, eventually established a new kingdom at Erebor, the Lonely Mountain. Khazad-dûm itself was left empty except for the Balrog and the other creatures that gradually entered its abandoned halls.

The Coming of Moria

After the Dwarves fled, the old name Khazad-dûm increasingly gave way in common usage to Moria, a Sindarin name commonly interpreted as the Black Pit or Black Chasm. The kingdom that had once been associated with wealth and craftsmanship became known instead for darkness and danger.

Orcs established themselves in the abandoned halls, especially as Sauron's influence grew again in Middle-earth. The enormous size of the underground realm meant that no single group occupied every passage or chamber, but parts of Moria became important Orc strongholds.

Durin's Bane remained in the depths throughout this period. For centuries, outsiders knew little about the nature of the terror that had driven the Dwarves away, and even the Wise did not immediately identify it as a Balrog of Morgoth.

Thrór Returns to Moria

By the late Third Age, Durin's Folk had suffered further disasters. Smaug captured Erebor in Third Age 2770, driving the Dwarves into exile and leaving their former king Thrór without a realm.

In 2790, Thrór traveled to Moria with his companion Nár. He entered the eastern gate alone, apparently hoping to reclaim something of the kingdom of his ancestors.

Inside Moria, Thrór was captured by Orcs led by Azog. Azog killed him, branded his own name upon Thrór's severed head, and threw it outside to Nár as a warning that no heir of Durin should attempt to reclaim the ancient kingdom.

The insult enraged the Dwarves and directly caused one of the largest wars they ever fought against the Orcs.

The War of the Dwarves and Orcs

When Thráin II, Thrór's son, learned of his father's death, he summoned the houses of the Dwarves to war. From Third Age 2793 to 2799, Dwarven armies attacked Orc strongholds throughout the Misty Mountains.

The campaign was long and brutal. The Dwarves gradually fought their way south until the war culminated at the Battle of Azanulbizar in 2799, fought in the Dimrill Dale before the eastern gate of Moria.

The battle caused enormous casualties on both sides. Náin, lord of the Iron Hills, challenged Azog and was killed, but Náin's son Dáin Ironfoot then confronted Azog and killed him. The Orc army was defeated, and the Dwarves achieved the revenge they had sought.

Victory, however, did not lead to the immediate restoration of Khazad-dûm.

Dáin's Warning

After the Battle of Azanulbizar, Thráin II wished to enter Moria and reclaim the ancestral kingdom. Dáin Ironfoot refused to support the attempt because he had looked through the eastern gate and sensed the presence of Durin's Bane.

Dáin understood that although the Orcs had been defeated, the deeper terror remained. The Dwarves therefore left Moria untouched and departed from the battlefield.

The losses had been so severe that the Dwarves could not bury all their dead in their customary manner. The fallen were burned upon great pyres, an extraordinary practice for a people who traditionally placed their dead in stone tombs.

Moria remained abandoned for nearly another two centuries.

Balin's Expedition

In Third Age 2989, Balin, one of the companions of Thorin Oakenshield and a veteran of the Quest of Erebor, led a new expedition to Moria. Nearly fifty years had passed since Erebor had been restored, and Balin hoped to establish a permanent Dwarven colony in the ancestral kingdom.

His companions included Óin and Ori, who had also traveled with Thorin, along with other Dwarves from Erebor. At first, the expedition appeared successful. The colonists entered Moria, defeated Orcs in the eastern sections, and established themselves in some of the old halls.

Balin took the title Lord of Moria, and messages initially passed between the colony and Erebor. For a time, it seemed possible that the Dwarves had succeeded where earlier generations had not.

Then communication ceased.

The Destruction of Balin's Colony

Balin's colony survived for approximately five years. In Third Age 2994, Balin went to look into the Mirrormere and was shot by an Orc archer. His body was recovered and buried in the Chamber of Mazarbul, where the surviving Dwarves erected a tomb.

After his death, the colony rapidly collapsed. Orcs attacked in increasing numbers, while the western exit was blocked by the Watcher in the Water. Óin was killed by the Watcher, and the surviving Dwarves were gradually trapped within the eastern parts of Moria.

Their final days were recorded in a damaged chronicle later called the Book of Mazarbul. The surviving entries described the loss of Balin, the closing of the exits, the advance of the Orcs, and the desperate final defense of the colony.

By the time anyone from outside learned their fate, every member of the expedition was dead.

The Fellowship Approaches Moria

In January 3019, the Fellowship of the Ring reached the western side of the Misty Mountains while traveling south from Rivendell. Their original plan was to cross by the Redhorn Pass near Caradhras, but severe weather forced them to abandon the attempt.

Moria became the principal alternative route. Gandalf was willing to attempt the passage, while Aragorn expressed serious concern because he had traveled through Moria before and feared what might await them there.

The Fellowship eventually reached the western gate and discovered the Doors of Durin. Gandalf initially misinterpreted the wording of the inscription before realizing that the command referred to the password itself.

He spoke the word mellon, and the doors opened.

The Watcher in the Water

The valley outside the western gate had changed greatly since the days of Khazad-dûm. Water had collected there, forming a dark pool in which a strange creature known as the Watcher in the Water had taken up residence.

As the Fellowship entered Moria, the Watcher attacked Frodo and attempted to drag him into the pool. The Company rescued him and passed through the doors, but the creature slammed the entrance shut and blocked it with debris.

The Fellowship could no longer retreat westward. They were forced to continue through Moria and seek the eastern gate.

Their journey brought them through vast halls, shafts, stairways, and passages that revealed the extraordinary scale of the ancient Dwarven kingdom. Even in ruin, Moria remained one of the greatest works of construction in Middle-earth.

The Chamber of Mazarbul

The Fellowship eventually reached the Chamber of Mazarbul, the old Chamber of Records. There they found the tomb of Balin and learned that the colony they had hoped might still exist had been destroyed years earlier.

They also discovered the Book of Mazarbul, badly damaged but still partly legible. Gandalf read from its final pages and reconstructed the collapse of the colony, including Balin's death, the attacks from the Orcs, and the loss of the western gate.

The last surviving entries described drums sounding in the deep and the defenders preparing for a final attack. Soon after Gandalf finished reading, the Fellowship heard those same drums.

Orcs attacked the chamber, and the Company was forced to fight its way out and continue toward the eastern side of Moria.

Durin's Bane Revealed

As the Fellowship fled toward the Bridge of Khazad-dûm, a more powerful presence approached behind the Orcs. The creature revealed itself as a Balrog of Morgoth, and at last the nature of Durin's Bane became clear.

The narrow bridge crossed a deep chasm and served as the final approach to the eastern gate. Gandalf remained behind while the rest of the Fellowship crossed, confronting the Balrog alone.

Gandalf broke the bridge beneath the creature and sent it falling into the abyss. As it fell, however, the Balrog's whip caught Gandalf and dragged him from the bridge.

The remaining members of the Fellowship escaped through the eastern gate into the Dimrill Dale, leaving Gandalf and the Balrog beneath the mountains.

Gandalf and the Balrog

The battle did not end with their fall. Gandalf later explained that he and the Balrog plunged into deep water beneath the roots of the mountains, where the creature's fire was extinguished but the struggle continued.

The Balrog fled through ancient passages far below the levels constructed by the Dwarves, and Gandalf followed it. Eventually they reached the Endless Stair, a legendary Dwarven stairway that rose from the deepest parts of Khazad-dûm to Durin's Tower on the summit of Celebdil.

There they fought again. After a prolonged battle, Gandalf cast the Balrog down from the mountain, and Durin's Bane was finally destroyed more than a thousand years after it had driven the Dwarves from Khazad-dûm.

Gandalf also died from the struggle, but he was sent back to Middle-earth as Gandalf the White to complete his task in the war against Sauron.

Moria After the Balrog

The death of Durin's Bane removed the greatest single obstacle to any future Dwarven restoration of Khazad-dûm. Even so, the kingdom was not immediately reclaimed during the War of the Ring.

Durin's Folk had their own battles to fight. In the north, King Dáin II Ironfoot was killed during the Battle of Dale while defending Erebor alongside King Brand, and the Dwarves remained occupied with the survival and recovery of their existing realms.

After Sauron's defeat, Dwarven communities continued to prosper in places such as Erebor and the Iron Hills. Gimli also established a new settlement in the Glittering Caves of Aglarond after the War of the Ring, showing that the Dwarves remained capable of founding new centers rather than immediately returning to Moria.

Tolkien does not describe a detailed restoration of Khazad-dûm during the early Fourth Age.

Durin VII and the Hope of Return

Dwarven tradition held that Durin would return among his people in later generations, and Tolkien's later chronological material refers to Durin VII, called Durin the Last. He was associated with the eventual restoration of Khazad-dûm, though the surviving details are sparse.

Because Tolkien never developed this later history fully, the exact date and circumstances of Moria's recolonization remain uncertain. What can be said is that the destruction of Durin's Bane made such a return possible in a way it had not been since Third Age 1980.

The hope of return therefore remained part of the Longbeards' relationship with Moria even after millennia of loss. Khazad-dûm was not merely an abandoned mine or ruined city to them; it was the ancestral heart of Durin's people.

The Dwarrowdelf

The history of Khazad-dûm stretched from the Elder Days into the Fourth Age and encompassed both the greatest achievements and the greatest disaster of Durin's Folk. It began with Durin beside the Mirrormere and grew into the largest and wealthiest Dwarven kingdom in Middle-earth, strengthened by refugees from Nogrod and Belegost and enriched by the discovery of mithril.

Its Second-Age history was marked by friendship with Eregion and resistance to Sauron. The Doors of Durin preserved the memory of cooperation between Narvi and Celebrimbor, while the survival of Khazad-dûm after Eregion's destruction demonstrated the strength of the Dwarven realm at its height.

The kingdom's downfall came not from Sauron or an invading army but from the awakening of a Balrog hidden beneath the mountains. The deaths of Durin VI and Náin I forced the Longbeards into exile, and neither the victory at Azanulbizar nor Balin's later colony was enough to restore the ancient realm.

When the Fellowship passed through Moria in 3019, they encountered the remains of all these layers of history at once. The western doors belonged to the friendship with Eregion, Balin's tomb belonged to the most recent attempt at restoration, the Orcs represented centuries of occupation, and Durin's Bane was the same creature that had destroyed the kingdom more than a thousand years earlier.

Gandalf's defeat of the Balrog finally ended the ancient threat beneath Khazad-dûm. Whether the Dwarves would fully restore their ancestral kingdom belonged to the history of a later age, but the principal cause of their exile had at last been removed.
